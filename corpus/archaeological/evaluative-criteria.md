# Evaluative Criteria — Archaeological Domain
# Mimbres Culture / Southwest Archaeology

**Status:** First draft — for expert review
**Purpose:** What specialists actually assess when analyzing a Mimbres ceramic. Organized by analytical domain.

---

## 1. Vessel Form and Morphology

**What to assess:**
- Rim diameter, vessel depth, wall curvature — these determine vessel class (bowl, olla, ladle) and place the object in the functional typology
- Wall thickness: consistent wall thickness across the vessel indicates skilled coil construction; variation signals production-stage issues or repair
- Rim form: direct rim, flared rim, insloping — each is typologically significant
- Base form: flat, convex, or rounded base affects use context

**Why it matters:** Form is diagnostic for typological placement and constrains functional interpretation. A bowl form used in burial contexts differs morphologically from one used for food preparation or serving.

**Key uncertainty:** Scale is often lost in photographs. Rim diameter estimation from image alone requires edge cues and contextual objects.

---

## 2. Construction Technique

**What to assess:**
- Evidence of coil-and-scrape construction: oblique striations on exterior, coil junctures visible in breaks or thin spots
- Paddle-and-anvil finishing: exterior surface compression patterns
- Interior scraping: tool marks from interior surface preparation
- Pinch or pull marks at rim indicating final shaping

**Why it matters:** Construction method is part of technological style — it encodes the learned sequence of the maker. Mimbres pottery is universally coil-built; departures suggest non-local production or a different tradition.

---

## 3. Surface Treatment and Slip Quality

**What to assess:**
- Slip application: even, well-adhered kaolin or chalky white slip vs. mottled, thin, or peeling slip
- Burnishing degree: highly burnished surfaces reflect light evenly; under-burnished surfaces show texture
- Slip coverage: full interior coverage vs. partial; exterior slip presence/absence
- Slip color: brilliant white (high kaolin content) vs. cream or buff (lower quality clay or preparation)

**Why it matters:** Slip quality is a direct indicator of production investment and skill. High-quality Mimbres pottery shows smooth, even, brilliantly white interior surfaces that enhance visual contrast with the black painted design. Poor slip preparation produces a degraded design field.

---

## 4. Paint Type and Application

**What to assess:**
- Mineral paint (iron-based): produces a fired, permanent black or dark brown line; tends toward matte or semi-matte finish; does not flake under normal conditions
- Organic/carbon paint (plant-based): produces a shiny black surface; can flake with age; technically distinct from mineral paint
- Line quality: fine, controlled lines vs. broad, uneven lines
- Paint density: consistent coverage within design areas vs. thin or blotchy fill
- Brush control: evidence of single-stroke line work vs. multiple strokes, hesitation marks, corrections

**Why it matters:** Paint type is a typological and dating indicator. Style III Classic Mimbres typically uses mineral paint; carbon paint appears in some vessels and in some periods. Line quality and brush control are direct evidence of production skill and design planning.

---

## 5. Design Execution Quality

**What to assess:**
- Symmetry precision: bilateral or rotational symmetry is deliberate; measure deviation from true symmetry as execution evidence
- Framing line integrity: the bounding lines that define the design field are typically the most controlled lines in Mimbres design; breaks or wobble indicate production issues
- Compositional logic: does the design fill the available field without crowding, awkward compression, or misjudged scale?
- Hatching regularity: parallel line work is technically demanding; even spacing and consistent line weight indicate skill
- Figure-ground balance: in figurative work, does the positive image and negative ground read as a resolved composition?

**Why it matters:** Design execution is the clearest available evidence of production skill and planning. Mimbres potters worked on curved surfaces with no guidelines; misjudgments in scale or placement accumulate and become visible in the finished vessel.

---

## 6. Iconographic Program

**What to assess:**
- Image type: geometric vs. figurative; if figurative, what species or figure type?
- Narrative vs. emblematic: does the image depict an action (hunting, movement, interaction) or present a figure as an emblem or identifier?
- Composite figures: human-animal hybrids or multi-species images — these carry higher interpretive weight and are rarer
- Design field organization: quartered, halved, continuous border, central medallion — how is the bowl interior organized?
- Completeness: is the iconographic program intact or is there damage that obscures the full image?
- Parallels: does this image appear in the published corpus? Unique images are more interpretively uncertain; images with corpus parallels can be read in relation to known examples.

**Why it matters:** The iconographic program is the primary evidence for cultural content interpretation. Figural imagery is what carries the most interpretive weight and what is most contested in the literature. Careful description before interpretation is essential.

---

## 7. Firing Quality

**What to assess:**
- Firing atmosphere: oxidizing (orange/tan exterior) vs. reducing/smudging (gray or black exterior); Mimbres pottery is typically fired in an oxidizing atmosphere
- Fire clouds: irregular dark patches on the exterior from uneven kiln conditions; minor fire clouds are typical; severe fire clouds suggest production issues
- Hardness: well-fired Mimbres pottery is hard and resonant; underfired pottery is soft and susceptible to erosion
- Spalling or peeling: evidence of thermal shock during firing

**Why it matters:** Firing is the final production variable that can degrade an otherwise well-made vessel. Fire cloud presence is normal and not a defect; severity matters.

---

## 8. Kill Hole

**What to assess:**
- Presence or absence
- Location: centered through the base vs. off-center; some images appear to have the kill hole deliberately placed through a meaningful part of the image (through a figure's body, through the center of a geometric design)
- Method: punched (impact from interior or exterior) vs. drilled; punching produces a different edge profile than drilling
- Timing: pre-firing (rare) vs. post-firing; post-firing is standard

**Why it matters:** Kill hole presence confirms burial context and is evidence for the ritual completion of the vessel. Placement relative to the image is an interpretive data point — deliberate placement through the image center or through a figure may carry meaning.

---

## 9. Provenience and Documentation Status

**What to assess:**
- Is the excavation context documented? (site, stratum, burial number, associated materials)
- Is the vessel from a published, peer-reviewed excavation report?
- Is this vessel potentially from looted context?

**Why it matters:** The majority of Mimbres pottery in collections was looted in the early 20th century before scientific excavation standards. Vessels without documented provenience carry significant interpretive limitations — associations with specific burials, household contexts, or temporal phases cannot be established. Analytical conclusions from unprovenanced material must be flagged as more speculative than those from documented contexts.

**Provenience scale:**
- Fully documented: excavated under controlled conditions, published report, associated materials known
- Partially documented: known site, uncertain stratum or burial association
- Undocumented: purchase or collection history only; site of origin unknown or unverified

---

## 10. Condition Assessment

**What to assess:**
- Post-depositional damage: soil erosion, root etching, mineral deposits
- Use wear: interior abrasion from food preparation or serving (pre-burial use evidence)
- Repair evidence: ancient repairs (pitch or resin fills, drilled holes for lacing breaks) indicate the vessel was valuable enough to repair
- Modern restoration: plaster fills, repainted areas — standard in museum collections but must be distinguished from original surface

**Why it matters:** Condition affects both what can be observed and what it means. Ancient repairs indicate in-life value. Modern restoration must be identified before making surface treatment or paint quality assessments.
