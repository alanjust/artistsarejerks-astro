# Tradition Identification Protocol — Southwest Ceramic Vessels

**Version:** 1
**Status:** First draft — paraphrase-supported throughout; primary source calibration pending
**Purpose:** Observable criteria for routing ceramic vessels to the correct Southwest tradition before tradition-specific evaluation begins. This gate prevents default attribution to Mimbres when observable features are ambiguous or point elsewhere.
**Scope:** Ceramic vessels only. Carved organic, composite, shell, and lithic objects have different diagnostic features — extend this protocol in future versions.

---

## Why this gate exists

The strongest observable features of most Southwest ceramic vessels — cream or white slip, dark painted design, hemispherical bowl form — are shared across multiple traditions. Without a disambiguation step, analysis defaults to whichever tradition dominates the reference class. In the current tool, that default is Mimbres. A well-made Ancestral Puebloan bowl and a well-made Mimbres bowl can look similar in a photograph. The features that distinguish them are specific and require active checking, not passive recognition.

Run this protocol before applying any tradition-specific evaluative criteria. Tradition-specific evaluation (Section A of the displacement block) produces wrong outputs when applied to the wrong tradition.

---

## Step 1 — The primary routing check: slip × paint program

The slip color and paint program combination is the most reliably observable distinguishing feature in photographs. Check this first.

### Routing matrix

| Slip color | Paint program | Route to |
|---|---|---|
| Brilliant white to cream | Single dark pigment (black-brown) | → **Mimbres or AP black-on-white disambiguation** (see Step 2) |
| Buff to gray-buff | Single warm pigment (red-orange) on buff ground | → **Hohokam Red-on-Buff** |
| White or cream | Two chemically distinct pigments (dark body + warm secondary, clearly different hue and saturation) | → **Multi-tradition check** (see Step 3) |
| White or cream | Three or more distinct color zones including red, black, and white | → **Polychrome traditions** (see Step 4) |
| Red slip | Various | → Narrow by form and design; could be several traditions |
| Unknown / ambiguous | — | → Flag explicitly; proceed with all candidate traditions |

**Critical note on the two-pigment check:** A two-pigment program is not a tonal variation of one pigment applied at different densities. It is two chemically and visually distinct materials — a dark brown-black body paint and a separately prepared warm orange, rust, or red-brown secondary paint — used in the same design program on the same object. If the secondary pigment reads as a clearly different hue from the primary (not just lighter or thinner), treat it as a distinct second pigment and run the multi-tradition check. Do not attribute to Classic Mimbres Black-on-white by default.

## Step 2 — Mimbres vs. Ancestral Puebloan black-on-white disambiguation

Both traditions use: white or cream slip, dark mineral paint, open bowl forms, and geometric or figurative design programs. This is the hardest disambiguation pair from image evidence alone. Check in the following order.

### 2a — Design system organization
- **Central medallion with open ground field, single large figure or geometric program filling the bowl interior** → Mimbres figurative or geometric convention
- **Band or register decoration organized around the vessel body or rim zone, with repeated units** → AP more typical; uncommon in Classic Mimbres interior-dominant program
- **Multiple framing lines, interlocking stepped geometric units, or fine parallel hatching in geometric fields** → AP black-on-white more likely (especially Chaco Black-on-white and Mesa Verde Black-on-white conventions)

### 2b — Figurative design conventions (when figurative content is present)
Mimbres-specific conventions documented in Brody's corpus:
- Single large animal or scene filling the bowl interior
- Checkerboard, hatching, or step-pattern body fill applied to figurative subjects
- Concentric-circle eye convention across multiple animal types
- Positive-negative ambiguity built into the geometric field
- Profile orientation with single visible wing/limb

AP figurative conventions are distinct and less systematically documented at this time. Key difference: Mimbres figurative conventions are highly codified and recognizable as a system. If the figurative program doesn't match the Mimbres conventions above, consider AP or other tradition.

### 2b-1 — AP-positive design indicators (observable from images)

The following features are positive indicators for AP attribution rather than Mimbres. None is individually conclusive, but two or more together make AP attribution well-supported.

**Band or register design organization.** AP black-on-white types consistently organize decoration as a band — a panel bounded above and below by framing lines, with geometric elements filling the band interior. When more than one framing line is present, they are typically of different thicknesses (Mesa Verde convention: thick line on top, thinner line below). Mimbres Classic geometric bowls use an interior-dominant composition without consistent register subdivision into bounded bands. If the bowl interior shows a clearly delimited design band with framing lines above and below, this is a strong AP indicator.

**Fine parallel hatching filling a geometric panel.** Evenly-spaced fine parallel lines used as the primary filler of triangular or rectangular geometric fields is characteristic of Chaco-tradition types. Mimbres uses hatching but typically as body fill on figurative subjects or in specific compositional roles—not as the dominant fill of abstract geometric panels. A bowl whose entire design vocabulary is built from hatched geometric fields points toward Chaco or Cibola White Ware types rather than Mimbres.

**All-over layout with quadrant subdivision.** Mesa Verde Black-on-white frequently employs an all-over layout in which the bowl interior is partitioned into two, three, or four symmetric quadrants, each filled with parallel arrangements of geometric elements. A single thick line near the rim serves as the only framing element. This radial-quadrant structure is visually distinct from the Mimbres central medallion.

**Rim ticking.** Mesa Verde Black-on-white characteristically marks flat bowl rims with short painted ticks, dots, or lines (perpendicular or parallel to the rim). This is a consistent type-specific feature. Classic Mimbres bowls have painted rim lines but not ticking in this sense. Rim ticking is a strong positive indicator for Mesa Verde-period AP.

**Vessel form diversity.** Mugs, kiva jars, pitchers (handled), and dippers alongside bowls strongly indicate AP. Mimbres production is overwhelmingly hemispherical open bowls. The mug form is AP-specific and has no Classic Mimbres equivalent.

**Paint character (secondary, not definitive from images).** Mesa Verde Black-on-white uses organic (carbon) pigment, which can read as softer, slightly browner, or less sharply reflective than the hard mineral black of Chaco and Mimbres. Chaco mineral paint is harder-edged. This distinction is real but cannot be confirmed from image evidence alone—organic vs. mineral paint identification requires physical or spectroscopic testing (pXRF, INAA). Treat as a supporting observation, not a primary criterion.

### 2b-2 — AP type-specific indicators by tradition and period

Once routed to AP, narrow by period and regional tradition using these criteria:

**Chaco and Cibola White Ware (~900–1150 CE):** Thin, hard walls; white paste (paste color often visible at breaks); mineral paint (sharp, hard black); crushed sherd temper (not visible from images); fine hatching as primary design filler; banded layout with multiple framing lines of varying weight; bowls and distinctive pitchers as common forms. Key types: Red Mesa Black-on-white, Gallup Black-on-white, Chaco Black-on-white. Period indicator: pre–1150 CE contexts.

**Northern San Juan / Mesa Verde (~1150–1280 CE):** Thick walls (noticeably heavier than Chaco or Mimbres); pearly-white, well-polished slip on both interior and exterior bowl surfaces; organic paint (slightly softer/faded purplish-black); flat rims with ticking; both banded and all-over (quadrant) layouts — with all-over layouts becoming dominant in later assemblages; stepped triangles, diamonds, interlocking elements, ticked lines as common elements; significant vessel form diversity (bowls, mugs, kiva jars, dippers). Key types: Mancos Black-on-white, McElmo Black-on-white, Mesa Verde Black-on-white. Period indicator: 1150–1280 CE; the last AP white ware tradition in this region before abandonment.

**Kayenta / Tusayan (~900–1300 CE):** Carbon pigment (dull black, slight brownish tint, occasionally slightly watery application); design vocabulary includes both curvilinear elements and geometric program — interlocking scrolls alongside solid triangles, parallel lines; geographic distribution centered in northeastern Arizona (Kayenta heartland) and Flagstaff region. Distinguishable from Mesa Verde by the presence of curvilinear and scroll elements in the geometric program. Key types: Black Mesa Black-on-white, Sosi Black-on-white, Tusayan Black-on-white.

**Source for AP type criteria:** New Mexico Office of Archaeological Studies, Southwest Ceramic Typology (ceramics.nmarchaeology.org) — peer-reviewed institutional typological descriptions. Mesa Verde Black-on-white: Wilson (2012). Chaco-McElmo Black-on-white: Wilson (2014). Broader AP survey: Cordell, *Archaeology of the Southwest* (1997) — primary source extraction pending Archive.org access.

### 2c — Two-pigment program
Classic Mimbres Black-on-white uses a single dark pigment on a white slip. A warm secondary pigment (rust-orange, red-brown) as a chemically distinct second color is not a standard Classic Mimbres Black-on-white feature. Its presence should trigger the multi-tradition check in Step 3, not Mimbres default attribution.

### 2d — Kill hole
Present → Supports Mimbres burial bowl attribution. Kill hole practice is documented as a Classic Mimbres burial custom (Shafer). It is not a pan-Southwest convention. Kill hole presence is a positive indicator for Mimbres; its absence is not diagnostic of any tradition.

### 2e — Paste color (when visible at breaks or chips)
Mimbres paste tends toward warm buff to reddish-brown. AP paste varies by region and period. This is a secondary check only — paste color alone is not diagnostic, and post-depositional staining can alter apparent paste color in photographs.

### 2f — When Mimbres vs. AP cannot be resolved from the image
State the ambiguity explicitly: "The slip, paint, and bowl form are consistent with both Classic Mimbres Black-on-white and Ancestral Puebloan black-on-white traditions. Definitive tradition attribution requires physical examination of paste and temper, and ideally provenience documentation."

Do not resolve the ambiguity by defaulting to Mimbres.

## Step 3 — Multi-tradition check for two-pigment programs

A two-pigment program — dark body color plus warm secondary pigment as distinct materials — opens the attribution to several traditions. Run all of these before attributing.

**Candidate traditions for a two-pigment program on a cream/white-slip vessel:**

**Some Classic Mimbres figurative bowls** do use two pigments for body differentiation (dark body mass, reddish-brown fringe or limb elements). This is documented but not standard. If the design program otherwise matches Mimbres conventions (central medallion, Mimbres figurative codes), Mimbres attribution may still be supported — but flag the two-pigment program as a distinguishing feature, not a confirming one.

**Ancestral Puebloan ceramic types** include multiple polychrome and bichrome traditions with secondary pigments. Depending on the period and region: Kayenta polychrome types, St. Johns Polychrome, and late prehistoric Hopi types (Sikyatki Polychrome) all use secondary pigments alongside a dark design pigment on white or cream slip.

**Post-Classic Southwest horizon** — after approximately 1150–1200 CE, many regional traditions moved toward multi-pigment programs. A two-pigment program on a vessel with other post-Classic indicators suggests a later date range than Classic Mimbres.

**Attribution from image evidence alone:** Narrow the candidates using design organization, vessel form, and design vocabulary. When candidates cannot be resolved, list them explicitly and state what physical analysis would distinguish them.

## Step 4 — Polychrome traditions

Three or more distinct color zones including combinations of black, red, white, and sometimes orange or yellow on a single vessel. Multiple distinct traditions produce polychrome ceramics in the prehistoric Southwest. Do not conflate them.

**Salado Polychrome horizon** (~1275–1450 CE): Gila, Tonto, and Pinto Polychrome types. Distributed across a large geographic area in the post-Classic period. The Salado horizon is a geographic distribution of a ceramic type, not a single ethnic group. Key visual markers: red, white, and black on buff; geometric designs with interlocking scrolls and hatched panels; characteristic vessel forms including jars with handles. Crown's *Ceramics and Ideology* (1994) is the reference.

**Casas Grandes / Ramos Polychrome** (~1200–1450 CE): Distinctive polychrome from the Paquimé sphere in northwestern Chihuahua. Key visual markers: geometric designs in black and red on white slip; macaw and serpent imagery; distinctive vessel forms including effigy vessels. The three-color program on Ramos Polychrome is visually distinct from Salado. Minnis and Whalen, *Ancient Paquimé and the Casas Grandes World* (2015), is the reference.

**Ancestral Puebloan polychrome types** (various periods): St. Johns Polychrome (~1175–1300 CE), Sikyatki Polychrome (late prehistoric/early historic Hopi). Sikyatki has naturalistic bird imagery and distinctive color program (yellow-orange slip with black and red design elements). If the vessel appears to have a yellow-orange rather than white slip ground, Sikyatki or Hopi tradition should be considered.

**Routing within polychrome:** Use vessel form, design vocabulary (geometric vs. figurative, specific motif types), and period indicators (post-Classic vs. Classic period). Polychrome vessels are more geographically and temporally variable than black-on-white types — state the candidate traditions and the evidence for each.

## Step 5 — Hohokam Red-on-Buff

If the routing matrix in Step 1 indicates Hohokam Red-on-Buff, check the following:

**Construction technique:** Hohokam primary production method is paddle-and-anvil, not coil-and-scrape. The exterior surface texture of paddle-and-anvil finished vessels differs from coil-and-scrape — exterior compression marks rather than oblique striations. This is often visible in photographs if the image quality is sufficient and the exterior is shown.

**Design vocabulary:** Hohokam Red-on-Buff design conventions include: life-form imagery (birds, lizards, snakes, human figures) rendered with distinctive stylized conventions different from Mimbres life-form conventions; geometric interlocking designs; scrolls. The movement and posture conventions in Hohokam life-form imagery are recognizably different from Mimbres figurative codes.

**Vessel forms:** Hohokam Red-on-Buff includes a broader range of vessel forms than Mimbres — not just open bowls but jars, effigy vessels, incense burners, trays. Vessel form is a secondary check after slip and paint.

**Period:** The Hohokam Red-on-Buff tradition spans roughly 300–1100 CE (Pioneer through Sedentary periods). Classic Hohokam (after ~1100 CE) shifts toward different ceramic types and social organization. A Red-on-Buff vessel is pre-Classic or Classic Hohokam.

**Reference:** Haury, *The Hohokam: Desert Farmers and Craftsmen* (1976) and the Snaketown assemblage at Arizona State Museum. (Paraphrase-supported at this time — primary source extraction pending.)

## Step 6 — When tradition cannot be determined

State explicitly that tradition cannot be determined from the image and list the candidate traditions with the specific evidence supporting each. Do not force attribution to the best-known or most-documented tradition. "Unknown / indeterminate" is a valid and often correct output.

The following always require physical examination or provenience documentation for confident tradition attribution:
- Any case where Mimbres and AP black-on-white cannot be distinguished from the observable features
- Any case where a two-pigment or polychrome program has multiple candidate traditions
- Any object with ambiguous paste/temper indicators
- Any object with no visible construction technique evidence

## Ambiguity protocol

When observable features are ambiguous between two or more traditions, use this framing:

> "The observable features — [list them] — are consistent with [Tradition A] and [Tradition B]. Confident attribution requires [specific evidence: paste and temper examination / provenience documentation / construction technique assessment from exterior view]. Current analysis proceeds on the working hypothesis that this object belongs to [most probable tradition based on available evidence], but this remains a provisional reading."

Do not omit the ambiguity statement. An uncertain attribution stated plainly is more useful to a researcher than a confident wrong one.

---

## Relationship to the displacement block

This protocol precedes Step 2 (Routing) in the displacement block. The routing step currently asks "which tradition is this?" without providing criteria to answer that question from observable evidence. This protocol provides those criteria.

After running this protocol, the tradition identified in Step 2 of the displacement block should be supported by at least two of the following: slip color, paint program, vessel form, design organization, construction technique evidence. If only one criterion supports the attribution, flag the attribution as provisional.
