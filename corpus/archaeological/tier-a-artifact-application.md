# Tier A Principles — Artifact Domain Application

**Version:** 1
**Date:** May 2026
**Status:** First draft — not yet tested against analysis outputs
**Purpose:** Translation layer between the 54 Hidden Grammar Principles (Tier A) and artifact analysis.

The 54 Principles describe perceptual mechanisms — what the human visual system does when
it encounters certain image features. Those mechanisms don't change depending on whether
the image is a Rothko or a Mimbres bowl. What changes is what those mechanisms are telling
you analytically.

This document reframes each relevant Tier A principle for artifact reading: when you
encounter this mechanism in an artifact image, here is what you are actually reading.

Do NOT use the studioTool framing from the 54 Principles when analyzing artifacts. That
framing asks what an artist did to a viewer. Artifact analysis asks what a mechanism
reveals about the object.

---

## Principles Active in Artifact Analysis

### Edge Detection
**54 Principles framing:** Controls where viewer attention lands. Hard vs. soft edges
create tension or calm. A studio tool for directing visual flow.

**Artifact framing:** Edge quality is production evidence. The character of a painted,
incised, or carved edge encodes information about the tool, the practitioner's skill level,
and the execution sequence.

For painted edges (ceramic): Is the line edge a single-stroke boundary or built up from
multiple passes? Single-stroke lines show consistent brush discipline — loaded brush, one
motion, no hesitation. Multiple-pass lines show correction, uncertainty, or deliberate
reworking. Hesitation marks (slight thickening or direction changes mid-line) are visible
evidence of the practitioner pausing. These are not aesthetic qualities — they are
production records.

For incised edges (carved, lithic, bone): Edge profile (V vs. U cross-section) indicates
tool type and pressure. Consistent profile throughout = single tool, consistent technique.
Profile changes within the same line = tool change, re-cutting, or different hand pressure
zones.

For ground edges (painted zone against unpainted surface): Crispness of the paint-to-ground
boundary encodes slip preparation quality and paint application control. Feathered or
irregular boundaries at the paint edge may indicate poor slip preparation, paint applied
over an insufficiently dry surface, or post-firing condition loss.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Line edge consistency: uniform throughout, or variable?
- Hesitation marks: thickening, wavering, direction micro-corrections
- Correction evidence: repainted areas, lines painted over
- Boundary quality where painted zone meets unpainted ground
- Whether edge quality varies across the design (higher investment zones often show finer
  edge control)

**Image ceiling:** Fine edge character requires sufficient image resolution. Low-resolution
or heavily compressed images may not preserve stroke-level detail. State when edge quality
assessment is limited by image resolution.

**Relevant object classes:** All painted surfaces; incised and carved organic; lithic

### Color Opponent Channels
**54 Principles framing:** Retina wired with three opponent pairs (red/green, blue/yellow,
black/white). Complementary colors create neural balance; analogous = harmony; clashing
combinations = dissonance.

**Artifact framing:** For polychrome objects, color zone organization is the primary
analytical question — which pairs are active, whether zone boundaries follow figure anatomy,
object structure, or an independent conventional scheme. For black-on-white ceramics
(Classic Mimbres, many Ancestral Puebloan types), only the black/white opponent channel
is active. This is the entire color system of the object. All design work — field
organization, figure-ground, investment gradient, iconographic program — is encoded
exclusively through luminance relationships in a two-value system.

Within a black/white-only system, the analytical questions shift: What is the painted-to-
unpainted ratio across the design field? Does the balance tip toward white-ground (light
field, dark figures) or toward black-ground (dark field, light negative shapes)? How does
this ratio vary between design zones — denser painting at the center vs. the rim? Does
hatching function as a mid-value gray through optical mixing, and if so what effective
value does it produce?

Any color that appears outside the dominant scheme — even trace amounts — is high-
significance evidence and should be noted specifically.

**What to look for in the image:**
- For polychrome: active color pairs, zone boundary logic (follows anatomy? object form?
  independent scheme?), unusual color departures from the dominant palette
- For black-on-white: painted-to-unpainted ratio overall and by zone; whether design
  tends toward white-ground or black-ground as dominant reading; effective gray values
  produced by hatching zones
- Where the color system departs from expectation

**Image ceiling:** Photography shifts color. Black-on-white analysis is relatively immune
(luminance relationship is preserved even through JPEG compression). Polychrome ceramics
can shift significantly under different photographic lighting — reds may read as oranges,
mineral paints may shift. State when color assessment may be compromised by photographic
conditions.

**Relevant object classes:** All; especially important for ceramic vessel analysis

### Figure-Ground Relationships
**54 Principles framing:** Brain separates figure from ground pre-consciously. Stable
figure-ground = easy to parse; ambiguous figure-ground = brain toggles between readings.
A compositional hierarchy tool.

**Artifact framing:** In Mimbres black-on-white ceramics, positive-negative ambiguity is
a documented cultural convention — Brody identifies deliberate figure-ground ambiguity as
a design strategy across the corpus. The painted element is not automatically the figure
and the unpainted element is not automatically the ground. Both can be designed as figures
simultaneously.

The analytical question is not "what is the figure" but "what is the figure-ground
relationship type in this design?" Three possibilities: (1) stable — one element clearly
reads as figure, the other as ground; (2) ambiguous — painted and unpainted areas toggle
equally; (3) programmatic — the design is structured so that figure-ground reading depends
on which design unit you are attending to, with deliberate variation across the field.

The kill hole's placement relative to the dominant figure is significant — it often
punctures the primary figure element rather than the ground, suggesting intentional
relationship.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Which element reads as figure on first contact?
- Does the reading toggle with sustained attention?
- Are there zones of stable figure-ground adjacent to zones of ambiguous figure-ground?
- Does the kill hole placement intersect the primary figure element?
- Do the unpainted (negative) shapes have as much regularity as the painted shapes?
  (If yes, both are planned as figures.)

**Image ceiling:** Figure-ground reading depends primarily on luminance relationship, which
photographs preserve well. Robust across image formats.

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels (especially Mimbres black-on-white);
incised and painted flat surfaces

### Grouping
**54 Principles framing:** Brain groups elements to reduce processing load. Proximity,
similarity, continuity, enclosure, and common fate all drive grouping. Brain wants pattern,
not chaos.

**Artifact framing:** How a design field is segmented into grouping units is a production
planning decision. The practitioner had to organize the full design space before beginning
to paint — grouping analysis recovers that plan. Primary grouping units (quarters, halves,
registers, border vs. field) reveal the first decisions made. Secondary units (repeated
motifs within each quarter) reveal the execution logic within each division.

Grouping by similarity reveals what elements were treated as equivalent production units —
what the practitioner considered "the same thing." Where similarity grouping breaks down
(an element that looks like it should be in a group but isn't) is evidence of either a
deliberate anomaly or a production inconsistency.

Grouping by enclosure (elements bounded by framing lines) reveals the hierarchical
structure of the design — what is inside and what is outside, what is primary field and
what is border element.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Primary field divisions: how is the interior divided first? Quarters, halves, radial?
- Repeated motif units: what is the repeating element, and how many times does it repeat?
- Where does grouping break down? Any element that doesn't fit the grouping scheme?
- Does grouping follow vessel structure (rim band as border) or impose an independent
  scheme over it?

**Relevant object classes:** All decorated surfaces; especially ceramic vessel interiors

### Overlap/Occlusion
**54 Principles framing:** When one form blocks another, brain reads spatial ordering.
Depth cue — Form A in front of Form B.

**Artifact framing:** In flat painted ceramic designs, overlap is primarily about design
layer sequence and composite figure structure, not depth. Where design elements overlap:
which was applied first (underlying) vs. second (overlying) — this is evidence of paint
application sequence. In some cases this can establish whether design elements are from
the same production session or from separate events (repair painting, later addition).

For composite figures (human-animal combinations in Mimbres): which body part overlaps
which determines compositional hierarchy and interpretive priority. The element that
overlaps others is typically the dominant identity of the figure. The overlapped elements
are typically appendages or secondary characteristics.

For multi-figure compositions: overlap between figures establishes whether they are
spatially layered (one in front of another) or whether the design is a flat arrangement
without spatial implication.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Where do design elements overlap? Which is on top?
- Can layer order establish paint application sequence?
- In composite figures: which body element overlaps which?
- In multi-figure scenes: is overlap present? Does it imply spatial depth or is it
  incidental contact?

**Image ceiling:** Paint layer order requires sufficient image quality to read. Very low
resolution obscures this. Confident paint layer sequence reading is uncertain without
physical examination or multispectral imaging.

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels; incised and painted flat surfaces;
composite objects

### Closure / Negative Space
**54 Principles framing:** Brain completes incomplete forms. Negative space is active shape
— the same neural machinery that detects edges also infers edges that aren't there.

**Artifact framing:** In Mimbres design specifically, the unpainted (negative) space is
a positive design element — Brody documents that unpainted zones are planned shapes, not
residual ground. The brain's closure mechanism is being actively engaged as a design
strategy: the unpainted space forms a shape that the visual system reads as a figure even
without a painted boundary.

Closure analysis asks: do the unpainted shapes have the same regularity as the painted
shapes? If yes, both are designed elements. If the unpainted shapes are irregular residuals,
they are incidental. This distinction is significant for understanding what the design
program actually contains.

For objects with attachment loss (missing insets, lost applied elements): closure is the
mechanism that makes absence readable. The visual system will attempt to complete the form
from surrounding context — which means absence-as-evidence (Principle 5 of the 15 Artifact
Principles) is cognitively readable, not just analytically reconstructed.

**What to look for in the image:**
- What shapes do the unpainted areas form? Are these regular or irregular?
- Do negative shapes have equal geometric precision to positive shapes?
- Where the design is incomplete: does closure produce a readable form from the partial
  information? What is that inferred form?
- Where elements are missing: can the surrounding design produce a closed form that
  implies what was there?

**Relevant object classes:** All decorated surfaces; especially relevant for Mimbres
black-on-white ceramics

### Simultaneous Contrast
**54 Principles framing:** Every color is processed relative to its neighbors, not in
isolation. Lateral inhibition means the same gray looks lighter against black and darker
against white.

**Artifact framing:** In black-on-white ceramics, simultaneous contrast is a functional
design mechanism, not an aesthetic effect. Dense hatching (many closely spaced lines) reads
as a gray field, lighter or darker depending on line width and spacing relative to
surrounding unpainted area. A practitioner who understands this effect can produce a range
of apparent values using only black paint by varying line density and width — no gray
pigment required.

The analytical question is: does the design show evidence of controlled value manipulation
through hatching density? If hatched zones produce consistent effective gray values across
the design field, that consistency is evidence of production planning for the simultaneous
contrast effect. If hatching density is inconsistent and produces uneven gray values, it
may indicate variable execution quality or an absence of explicit planning for this effect.

Adjacent painted zones also affect each other. A thick black line running alongside a thin
black line will read as darker/lighter relative to its neighbor than either line reads in
isolation.

**What to look for in the image:**
- In hatched areas: what effective gray value does the hatching produce? Is it consistent
  across the design?
- Where different line densities are adjacent: does the apparent value relationship suggest
  deliberate contrast management?
- Are there zones where contrast relationships produce visual effects that seem unplanned
  (unintended darkening or lightening)?

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels with hatching; incised surfaces; woven
textiles with patterned density variation

### Specularity / Surface Reflection
**54 Principles framing:** Specularity signals surface hardness, wetness, material type.
Brain reads material properties from highlight character before conscious identification.

**Artifact framing:** Surface reflectance in ceramic analysis distinguishes burnished from
matte surfaces, slip from unslipped clay body, and sometimes mineral from carbon paint.
Mineral paint is typically matte and permanent; carbon (organic) paint can be somewhat
shiny and may flake over time. Where photographic conditions preserve specularity
information, it can help distinguish surface treatments without physical examination.

Differential reflectance across the object surface is the key observation: if the painted
zones and unpainted zones show different reflectance, that is evidence of separate surface
preparation events. If the entire surface reflects uniformly, that suggests overall surface
treatment (total burnishing) before painting.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Differential surface reflectance: do painted and unpainted zones reflect differently?
- Does reflectance pattern follow the design or the clay surface texture?
- Any zones of specular highlight that would indicate polished or highly burnished areas?
- Evidence of matte vs. shiny paint (where image conditions permit this distinction)

**Image ceiling:** Photography frequently obscures or distorts specularity. Flash
photography flattens it; raking light exaggerates it; digital compression can eliminate
it entirely. A confident specularity reading requires knowing the photographic conditions
and is often not achievable from a standard collection photograph. State explicitly when
specularity assessment is unreliable.

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels; carved organic with applied pigment;
shell artifacts

### Visual Pop-out / Pre-attentive Features
**54 Principles framing:** Unique features grab attention before conscious processing —
in parallel, under 200ms. Pre-attentive features: unique color in a field of similar colors,
unique orientation in a field of parallel lines, unique size, unique shape.

**Artifact framing:** In artifact analysis, pop-out elements are evidence of intentional
differentiation in the design program — elements that break from the surrounding pattern
to signal significance. Pop-out is not accidental in a tradition with strong production
conventions: a practitioner working within established conventions who introduces a
departing element is making a specific design decision.

The kill hole is the most unambiguous pop-out element in a Mimbres bowl: it is a singular
disruption of an otherwise continuous surface. Its position relative to the dominant design
element is analytically significant — a kill hole that appears to deliberately intersect
a figure's head or vital zone is doing something different from a kill hole in a neutral
ground area.

Other pop-outs to identify: a figure that breaks from the bilateral or radial symmetry
scheme; a single unpainted area in an otherwise fully painted zone; a unique scale element
in a field of uniform-scale elements; a single figure in a field of geometric elements or
vice versa.

**What to look for in the image:**
- What element, if any, pops out from the surrounding pattern?
- Kill hole: where is it positioned relative to the primary figure? Does it appear to
  deliberately intersect a specific design element?
- Any element that breaks from the repeating pattern: is it localized (single instance)
  or systematic (rule-governed variation)?
- Does the pop-out element align with the design's iconographically significant zone?

**Relevant object classes:** All decorated surfaces; kill hole analysis for Mimbres
ceramic vessels

### Face Detection
**54 Principles framing:** Fusiform Face Area grabs faces pre-attentively. Pareidolia is
the face detection system being overly trigger-happy.

**Artifact framing:** For Southwest artifacts with anthropomorphic or zoomorphic imagery,
face detection analysis asks: is a face depicted, what type (human, animal, composite),
what are the structural face elements (eyes, mouth, nose markers), and what does their
arrangement signal?

In Mimbres composite figures — beings combining human and animal characteristics — the face
element typically establishes the figure's primary identity. A quadruped body with a human
face = human-identified; a human body with a horned animal face = animal or supernatural-
identified. The face is where the identity claim is made.

Eye type and direction matter: dot eyes vs. outlined eyes vs. naturalistic eyes carry
different information about the depicted being's category (animal, human, supernatural).
Eyes directed toward the kill hole location is documented in some Mimbres examples — if
present, note it explicitly.

Pareidolic face readings in geometric designs are possible. If a geometric pattern
triggers face detection: note whether this appears intentional (geometric elements
arranged to produce reliable face reading) or incidental. Intentional pareidolic design
is documented in several Southwest traditions.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Is a face depicted? Locate and describe it.
- Face type: human, animal, composite?
- Eye type: dot, outlined, naturalistic, absent?
- Gaze direction if determinable
- Does any figure's gaze vector point toward the kill hole?
- Do any geometric elements produce reliable pareidolic face readings?

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels with figurative programs; carved organic
effigies; composite objects

### Biological Motion Detection
**54 Principles framing:** Visual system specializes in animate movement patterns.
Dynamic posture and gesture read as alive vs. static. Implies living intention.

**Artifact framing:** For figurative imagery in Southwest ceramics, biological motion
distinguishes active depictions from emblematic depictions. Mimbres figurative bowls
commonly show animals and figures in naturalistic action poses (running, swimming, hunting,
feeding, engaged in specific behaviors) and in emblematic static poses (frontal, symmetrical,
display position). Biological motion is the mechanism that reads which type is present.

This distinction carries interpretive weight: active pose = narrative or scene depiction
(something is happening); static/emblematic pose = categorical or symbolic depiction
(this is a type, not an event). Both are documented design strategies in Mimbres, and
both have different implications for iconographic analysis.

Multi-figure compositions with coordinated motion patterns — figures moving in concert,
pursuing each other, engaged in parallel action — are a distinct category. Common fate
(shared directional motion) is the grouping mechanism that binds these into a scene.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Is the depicted figure in an active pose or a static/emblematic pose?
- If active: what action is being depicted? What behavior does the body position indicate?
- If static: is the figure in a display orientation (frontal, symmetrical) or a resting
  orientation?
- In multi-figure compositions: are figures engaged in coordinated action or positioned
  independently?

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels with figurative programs; carved organic
effigies

### Gaze Direction / Social Attention
**54 Principles framing:** Where depicted figures look directs viewer attention. Social
attention mechanism is hardwired — gaze following begins in infants at 3 months.

**Artifact framing:** In Southwest figurative ceramics, gaze direction is evidence about
scene composition and figure-to-figure social relationships — whether figures are aware
of each other, engaged in a common focus, or positioned without social relationship.

Direct viewer-gaze (figure looking out of the bowl at the person viewing it) is relatively
rare in Mimbres — most figures engage with the scene or have no determinable gaze. When
present, direct gaze is significant enough to note explicitly: it changes the relationship
between object and viewer.

Gaze toward the kill hole location is the most analytically significant gaze observation
in Mimbres analysis. If a figure's eyes are directed toward the kill hole, note the spatial
relationship precisely: is the figure facing the hole, adjacent to it, or oriented at an
angle?

In multi-figure compositions: do figures gaze toward each other (social interaction scene)
or in a common direction (shared focus scene)? These are structurally different composition
types.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Do depicted figures have visible eyes with determinable gaze direction?
- What direction is the gaze vector — toward other figures, toward kill hole, outward?
- In multi-figure: are gaze directions coordinated or independent?
- Is direct viewer-gaze present? If yes, note it explicitly.

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels with figurative programs; carved organic
effigies with face elements

### Common Fate
**54 Principles framing:** Elements moving together group perceptually. Shared direction
and trajectory create unity even across shape and color differences.

**Artifact framing:** In geometric ceramic design, common fate reveals directional
organization — whether design elements are organized into unified directional units.
Parallel bands, spirals rotating in a common direction, hatching lines sharing orientation:
these are evidence that elements were executed together, in the same production sequence,
in the same motion. Reversals in directional orientation are either planned design contrasts
or evidence of separate execution passes.

In figurative compositions with multiple figures, common fate is what produces a "scene" —
multiple figures moving in coordinated directions, pursuing or fleeing, engaged in parallel
action. This is the grouping mechanism that binds individual figures into a narrative unit.

**What to look for in the image:**
- Do geometric elements share directional orientation? What direction?
- Where do directional orientations change? Is the change abrupt or graduated?
- In hatching: are lines within a field all parallel (single-pass execution) or mixed
  orientation (multiple passes)?
- In figurative multi-figure compositions: do figures share a motion direction?

**Relevant object classes:** Ceramic vessels (geometric and figurative); incised surfaces;
woven textiles

## Tier A Principles with Limited or No Application to Artifact Analysis

These principles are active in the fine art domain but have restricted application when
analyzing archaeological artifacts. Do not apply them as primary analytical tools unless
the specific condition noted is present.

**Atmospheric Perspective:** Not applicable to flat ceramic design surfaces. Apply only
when analyzing a three-dimensional object in a full photographic context where depth
recession is present, or when a figurative composition explicitly depicts spatial recession.

**Linear Perspective:** Not applicable to Mimbres or most Southwest ceramic flat design
fields. Apply only if a figurative scene explicitly depicts architectural space or spatial
recession with convergence. Absence of linear perspective in Southwest ceramic design
is the norm, not a deficiency.

**Elevation in Picture Plane:** Not applicable to ceramic bowl interiors read from above
(no top/bottom orientation in the primary viewing axis). Apply only when a composition
has an explicit up/down orientation, such as a wall panel, a flat tablet, or a figurative
scene with an implied ground plane.

**Binocular vs. Monocular Depth Cues:** Not applicable to flat painted design analysis.
Apply to three-dimensional object form reading — when assessing the three-dimensional
form of a figurine, effigy vessel, or carved object from photographic views.

**Light Source Logic:** Not applicable to painted ceramic designs that do not model
lighting. Apply to carved forms, figurines, and three-dimensional objects where light and
shadow encode three-dimensional structure, and where photographic lighting conditions
permit assessment.

## Application Hierarchy by Object Class

**Classic Mimbres black-on-white ceramic bowls:**
Primary: Edge Detection, Figure-Ground, Color Opponent Channels (black/white channel only),
Closure/Negative Space, Simultaneous Contrast, Visual Pop-out, Grouping
Secondary (figurative only): Face Detection, Biological Motion Detection, Gaze Direction,
Common Fate, Overlap/Occlusion

**Polychrome ceramics (Hohokam, Salado, Casas Grandes):**
Primary: Color Opponent Channels (full palette), Edge Detection, Grouping, Figure-Ground
Secondary: Simultaneous Contrast, Visual Pop-out, Overlap/Occlusion

**Carved organic objects (wood effigies, bone tools):**
Primary: Edge Detection, Specularity, Biological Motion Detection, Face Detection
Secondary: Overlap/Occlusion, Symmetry (via Artifact Principle 8)

**Shell artifacts:**
Primary: Edge Detection (incised design), Color Opponent Channels (natural shell banding),
Specularity (shell surface), Visual Pop-out (carved relief elements)

**Lithic objects:**
Primary: Edge Detection (flake scars, incised marks), Specularity (surface polish),
Visual Pop-out (distinctive elements)

**Composite/ritual objects:**
Full set may apply depending on component materials. Analyze each material component
using its applicable class hierarchy.
