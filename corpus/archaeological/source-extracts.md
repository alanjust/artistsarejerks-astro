# Mimbres Culture / Southwest Archaeology

## J.J. Brody — *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (1977, revised 2004)

[PARAPHRASE] On the distinctiveness of Classic Mimbres imagery: Brody argues that Mimbres figurative pottery has no close parallel in the pre-Columbian Southwest. The naturalistic rendering of animals and humans — fish, rabbits, deer, insects, composite beings — in combination with the precision of the geometric design tradition represents a synthesis that appears rapidly in the archaeological record and ends equally abruptly. The imagery is not a casual decorative program but a systematic visual language, and analysis requires treating the full corpus as the reference frame rather than individual vessels in isolation.

[PARAPHRASE] On interpretation: Brody is explicit that the meaning of Mimbres imagery cannot be fully recovered. The people who made it are gone; there are no direct ethnographic descendants who can explain the imagery from the inside. He argues for formal and comparative analysis as the appropriate method — identifying recurring figures, relationships between figures, compositional conventions — while maintaining skepticism about meaning claims that exceed the visual evidence.

[PARAPHRASE] On style sequence: The transition from geometric Style II to figurative Style III is not a break but a continuation — the underlying design logic (framing lines, symmetry systems, field organization) persists across the transition. The figurative imagery occupies the same compositional framework as the geometric designs that preceded it.

## Harry Shafer — *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (2003)

[PARAPHRASE] On use before burial: NAN Ranch data shows that the majority of Classic Mimbres bowls placed in burials bear use wear consistent with extended domestic use — interior abrasion from food preparation, sooting on exterior bases. The burial context is an endpoint, not the original purpose. This challenges the assumption that elaborately decorated vessels were made specifically as mortuary goods and argues instead that they were valued household objects whose final act was placement with the dead.

[PARAPHRASE] On production sequence: Shafer documents the full chaîne opératoire of Mimbres pottery — clay procurement, temper selection, coil construction, scraping and smoothing, slip application, design painting, firing. Each step has observable signatures in the finished vessel. The production sequence was learned through apprenticeship and encoded culturally as technological style.

[PARAPHRASE] On craft specialization: NAN Ranch evidence suggests household production rather than specialized workshops, at least through most of the Classic period. Multiple households within the site were producing pottery. This has implications for how to read variability in design quality — it is partly a function of individual skill and household tradition, not a workshop hierarchy.

## Michelle Hegmon — on ceramic variability and social organization

[PARAPHRASE] Hegmon argues that ceramic variability in the Mimbres tradition encodes social information at multiple scales — community identity, household identity, and individual production practice. The question is not "what does this image mean" in an iconographic sense but "what social work is this vessel doing." Pottery circulated within and between communities; its design carried signals about the producer's social position and affiliations.

[PARAPHRASE] On the limits of individual expression as an analytical category: Applying Western concepts of individual artistic expression to Mimbres pottery production misframes the question. The maker was operating within strong cultural conventions about what a correct Mimbres bowl looked like. Variation within those conventions is real but is better understood as social signal than as personal artistic statement.

## Barbara Moulard — on narrative sequences

[PARAPHRASE] Moulard argues that many Mimbres figurative images are episodes in narrative sequences — single frames from stories that would have been legible to their original audience. The fish with a human in its belly, the deer with arrows, the hunting scene — these are not emblems but moments. Their full meaning depends on knowing the story, which we do not. But the narrative structure is recoverable through formal analysis: sequential relationships between figures, directional cues, implied action.

[PARAPHRASE] On composite figures: Human-animal composite beings appear across the Mimbres corpus. Moulard reads these as figures at the boundary between the human and animal worlds — shamanic transformation, spirit helpers, or mythological characters who partake of both. These figures are among the most significant iconographically and the most interpretively difficult.

## Steven LeBlanc — on the Classic Mimbres collapse

[PARAPHRASE] LeBlanc documents evidence of conflict at Classic Mimbres sites — skeletal trauma, defensive site positioning, evidence of burning and abandonment. The collapse of Classic Mimbres culture around 1130–1150 CE was rapid, not gradual. He argues against purely environmental explanations (though drought played a role) in favor of a model that includes demographic pressure and inter-community violence. Understanding the end context is relevant to interpreting the ceramic record — the intensification of elaborate mortuary pottery may correspond to a period of social stress.

## Methodological vocabulary to draw on

The following terms appear consistently in Mimbres ceramic scholarship. Use them precisely:

- **Provenience** (not provenance — in archaeology, provenience = spatial context within a site; provenance = ownership history. These are distinct.)
- **Typological placement** — assigning a vessel to a recognized ceramic type within the sequence
- **Chaîne opératoire** — the full production sequence from raw material to finished object
- **Kill hole** — deliberate post-firing perforation through the vessel base; associated with burial practice
- **Firing atmosphere** — oxidizing vs. reducing conditions in the kiln, producing different surface colors
- **Slip** — the thin clay wash applied to the vessel surface before painting; a distinct layer from the vessel body
- **Mineral paint** vs. **carbon/organic paint** — two distinct paint technologies with different visual and analytical signatures
- **Iconographic program** — the full design system of a vessel, treated as an integrated whole
- **Composite figure** — a depicted being combining human and animal characteristics
- **In situ** — found in original depositional context, undisturbed
- **Technological style** — the culturally encoded choices made throughout the production sequence, readable as social identity markers
