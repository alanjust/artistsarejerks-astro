## Mimbres / Mogollon Specialists

### J.J. Brody — *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (1977, revised 2004)

[PARAPHRASE] On the distinctiveness of Classic Mimbres imagery: The naturalistic rendering of animals and humans in combination with the precision of the geometric design tradition represents a synthesis that appears rapidly in the archaeological record and ends equally abruptly. The imagery is not casual decoration but a systematic visual language requiring corpus-level analysis rather than vessel-by-vessel interpretation.

[PARAPHRASE] On the limits of interpretation: The meaning of Mimbres imagery cannot be fully recovered. Formal and comparative analysis is the appropriate method — identifying recurring figures, relationships between figures, compositional conventions — while maintaining skepticism about meaning claims that exceed the visual evidence.

[PARAPHRASE] On style sequence: The transition from geometric Style II to figurative Style III is not a break but a continuation — the underlying design logic (framing lines, symmetry systems, field organization) persists across the transition.

### Harry Shafer — *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (2003)

[PARAPHRASE] On use before burial: The majority of Classic Mimbres bowls placed in burials bear use wear consistent with extended domestic use — interior abrasion, sooting on exterior bases. The burial context is an endpoint, not the original purpose.

[PARAPHRASE] On production sequence: Each stage of the chaîne opératoire leaves observable signatures in the finished vessel. The production sequence was learned through apprenticeship and encoded culturally as technological style.

### Michelle Hegmon — on ceramic variability and social organization

[PARAPHRASE] Ceramic variability encodes social information at multiple scales — community identity, household identity, individual production practice. The question is not what does this image mean iconographically but what social work is this vessel doing. This framework transfers to non-ceramic objects: any material culture item's formal conventions are social information.

[PARAPHRASE] Applying Western concepts of individual artistic expression to prehistoric production misframes the question. The maker operated within strong cultural conventions. Variation within those conventions is social signal, not personal artistic statement.

### Barbara Moulard — on narrative sequences

[PARAPHRASE] Many Mimbres figurative images are episodes in narrative sequences — single frames from stories that would have been legible to their original audience. Their full meaning depends on knowing the story, which we do not. Narrative structure is recoverable through formal analysis even when specific content is not.

### Steven LeBlanc — on the Classic Mimbres collapse

[PARAPHRASE] The collapse of Classic Mimbres culture around 1130–1150 CE was rapid, not gradual. Evidence of conflict — skeletal trauma, defensive site positioning, evidence of burning — argues against purely environmental explanations. Post-Classic material from the Mimbres area exists in a fundamentally different social landscape.

## Broad Southwest Scholars

### Linda Cordell — *Archaeology of the Southwest* (1997)

[PARAPHRASE] The Southwest is not a uniform culture area but a mosaic of interacting regional traditions. Hohokam, Mogollon, and Ancestral Puebloan trajectories share some processes — aggregation, architectural elaboration, post-Classic reorganization — but maintain distinctive material culture sequences that should not be flattened into a single narrative.

[PARAPHRASE] On the post-Classic reorganization (13th–14th century): The period 1150–1350 CE saw major population movements, village aggregation, and the abandonment of many Classic-period centers across the Southwest. Post-Classic material culture reflects these reorganizations — objects from this period exist in transformed social landscapes, and Classic-period interpretive frameworks may not apply without modification.

### Polly Schaafsma — on iconographic continuity

[PARAPHRASE] The same iconographic vocabulary appears across ceramic, rock art, architectural mural, and portable object contexts in the Southwest, spanning multiple centuries and cultural traditions. Specific motifs — the horned serpent (Avanyu), mountain lion, rain/cloud imagery, warrior/shield figures — appear in Hohokam, Mimbres, Ancestral Puebloan, and Casas Grandes contexts. This cross-tradition, cross-media continuity argues for shared cosmological frameworks rather than independent invention in each tradition.

[PARAPHRASE] On Kachina cult origins: Proto-Kachina imagery is identifiable in rock art and ceramics from approximately 1275 CE onward, with a geographic distribution suggesting emergence in the Rio Grande/upper Gila area before spreading. Objects with Kachina-related imagery postdating 1275 CE must be considered in relation to this emerging ritual complex.

### Patricia Crown — *Ceramics and Ideology* (1994)

[PARAPHRASE] The Salado Polychrome horizon (ca. 1275–1450 CE) was not the result of population movement but the spread of a shared ritual/ideological complex through ceramic conventions. Similar polychrome designs appearing across a broad geographic area indicate shared belief systems, not shared ancestry. This argument establishes the principle that ceramic conventions can carry ideological content independently of population movement — important for interpreting any widely distributed ceramic tradition.

## Exchange and Material Culture Scholars

### Kate Spielmann — on craft production and exchange

[PARAPHRASE] Full-time craft specialization was rare in the prehistoric Southwest. Most objects, including elaborately decorated ceramics and ornaments, were produced by part-time specialists operating within household or community contexts. True specialization — defined by full-time production for exchange — requires specific evidence: standardization measurable against a comparative sample, production debris in dedicated workshop spaces, and distribution patterns suggesting market or redistribution exchange. Without this evidence, "specialist production" should be used cautiously.

[PARAPHRASE] On the social embedding of production: Craft production in the Southwest was not economically driven but socially embedded — objects were produced within relationships of obligation, ceremony, and community membership. The distribution of objects reflects social relationships as much as or more than economic efficiency.

### Phil Weigand / Garman Harbottle — on turquoise exchange

[PARAPHRASE] Compositional analysis (INAA) of Southwest turquoise identifies multiple distinct source areas: Cerrillos (New Mexico), Mineral Park and Kingman (Arizona), Battle Mountain and Royston (Nevada), and others. Different sources dominated different periods and regions. Cerrillos turquoise dominates Chaco-affiliated contexts; Nevada sources appear more frequently in later periods and western traditions. Without compositional analysis, turquoise source attribution from visual inspection alone is not reliable. Its presence indicates exchange participation; the specific network requires analysis to identify.

[PARAPHRASE] Turquoise at Pueblo Bonito — more than 50,000 pieces in a single cache deposit — represents an accumulation strategy that implies long-term controlled access to turquoise sources and exchange networks. The scale is qualitatively different from typical site-level turquoise quantities and signals a distinct social position for Chaco in the turquoise distribution network.

### Kelley Hays-Gillis — on Southwest textiles

[PARAPHRASE] Fiber production — basketry, cordage, and textiles — was technically sophisticated across all Southwest traditions. The systematic absence of fiber from most archaeological assemblages is entirely taphonomic: organic materials decompose in most depositional contexts. Dry cave preservation is exceptional, not representative. The surviving record is a biased sample of what was undoubtedly ubiquitous production. This has direct implications for analysis: the absence of fiber evidence at a site is not evidence of absence of fiber production.

[PARAPHRASE] On dyed fiber: Plant and mineral dye technology was present across Southwest traditions. Blue and blue-green dye in prehistoric fiber is documented but uncommon — indigo (cultivated or traded) and larkspur are identified sources. The presence of blue-green dyed fiber in a composite object is significant because it represents an investment in color specificity beyond utilitarian need.

## Hohokam Specialists

### Emil Haury — on Snaketown and Hohokam sequences

[PARAPHRASE] The Hohokam sequence at Snaketown establishes the full chronological range of Hohokam material culture. The shell ornament industry — producing Glycymeris bracelets, frog effigies, pendants, and beads from Gulf of California shell — was a Hohokam specialty beginning in the Pioneer period and continuing through the Classic. The scale of shell processing debris at Snaketown indicates production well beyond local demand — suggesting redistribution into broader exchange networks.

### Paul Minnis and Michael Whalen — on Casas Grandes / Paquimé

[PARAPHRASE] Paquimé was a locally emergent regional center in the Casas Grandes valley, not a Mesoamerican colony or a Chacoan expansion. Its distinctive material culture — Ramos Polychrome ceramics, copper bells, macaw husbandry facilities, T-shaped doorways — represents a synthesis of Southwestern and northern Mexican traditions. The scale of macaw and turkey husbandry at Paquimé (hundreds of birds in dedicated pens) and the evidence for copper bell production indicate specialist production for redistribution into a regional exchange network.

[PARAPHRASE] On Paquimé's collapse (~1450 CE): The site was violently abandoned. Evidence of burning, unburied human remains, and the rapid termination of production activities suggests conflict rather than gradual abandonment. Post-Paquimé material culture in the Casas Grandes valley shows distinct changes.

## Field Vocabulary — Complete Reference

The following terms appear consistently in Southwest archaeological scholarship. Use them precisely:

**Provenience** (not provenance) — spatial context within a site (provenience = where it was found; provenance = ownership history; these are distinct and must not be conflated)

**Chaîne opératoire** — the full production sequence from raw material selection through finished object; each stage leaves observable signatures

**Kill hole** — deliberate post-firing perforation through a ceramic vessel base; associated with Mimbres burial practice specifically; not a general Southwest term

**Slip** — thin clay wash applied to vessel surface before painting; a distinct layer from the vessel body

**Mineral paint** vs. **carbon/organic paint** — two distinct ceramic paint technologies with different visual and analytical signatures

**Typological placement** — assigning an object to a recognized type within a regional sequence

**Composite figure** — depicted being combining human and animal characteristics; interpretively significant in both ceramic and non-ceramic contexts

**Iconographic program** — the full visual system of an object, treated as an integrated whole

**Technological style** — culturally encoded production choices readable as social identity markers across all material categories, not just ceramics

**In situ** — found undisturbed in original depositional context

**Cache** — deliberately assembled group of objects intentionally deposited together; distinct from burial assemblage and from midden

**Subtractive carving** — material removed from a blank to produce form; opposite of additive/built construction

**Inset element** — material set into a prepared cavity in a substrate (turquoise eye in a wood effigy); distinct from applied surface element

**Pelage** — animal coat/fur pattern; relevant for effigy color zone analysis

**Umbo** — the hinge/beak area of a bivalve shell; the primary location for carved effigy figures on Hohokam Glycymeris bracelets

**pXRF** — portable X-ray fluorescence; non-destructive compositional analysis tool used for source attribution of metals, obsidian, pigments, and other materials

**INAA** — Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis; destructive compositional analysis providing precise elemental fingerprints; the gold standard for turquoise and ceramic paste sourcing

**Taphonomy** — the processes affecting an object between deposition and discovery (decay, animal disturbance, soil chemistry, human disturbance); taphonomic reasoning distinguishes absence-of-evidence from evidence-of-absence

**Post-Classic** — in Southwest context, roughly post-1150 CE (after Classic Mimbres collapse and Chaco decline); a period of significant regional reorganization; objects from this period exist in a transformed social landscape

**Salado horizon** — the geographic distribution of Salado Polychrome ceramics (~1275–1450 CE); an ideological/ritual horizon, not an ethnic group

## Society for American Archaeology — Principles of Archaeological Ethics (2024)
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: SAA, *Principles of Archaeological Ethics*, adopted March 7, 2024. PDF: https://documents.saa.org/container/docs/default-source/doc-careerpractice/saa_ethics.pdf

**Directly relevant to corpus use — ethical constraints on analysis:**

On commercialization (Principle No. 3):
> "The commercial sale or authentication of archaeological objects and other data for profit must not be practiced by archaeologists."
> "Archaeology should engage with the community to deter looting, marketing, and the selling of artifacts by framing the archaeological record as an invaluable and nonrenewable resource for telling the story of the past and present."

On stewardship (Principle No. 1):
> "As stewards, archaeologists are both caretakers of and advocates for the archaeological record."
> "Archaeologists work for the long-term conservation and protection of the archaeological record, while respecting the rights and beliefs of Indigenous and descendant peoples to the representation of their heritage as manifested in the archaeological record."

On public engagement (Principle No. 4):
> "Archaeologists (1) explain what an archaeologist does in culturally appropriate ways; (2) explain the importance of understanding, preserving, protecting, and interpreting the past for the present and future; and (3) listen to and incorporate the knowledge and concerns of impacted communities."

**Evaluative implications for this corpus:**

The SAA principles establish hard stops for analysis:
- Do not authenticate objects for commercial purposes
- Do not assign market value or rarity rankings
- Treat the archaeological record as a nonrenewable resource, not a commodity
- Acknowledge Indigenous and descendant community rights to representation
- Flag any object with NAGPRA sensitivity before proceeding with standard analysis

These constraints belong in **STEP 0** of `evaluative-criteria.md` — they are gates, not criteria. An object that fails STEP 0 (sacred, funerary, NAGPRA-covered, market-authentication context) gets a different response than one that passes through to standard object-class routing.

## Kennett et al. — "Archaeogenomic Evidence Reveals Prehistoric Matrilineal Dynasty"
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: Douglas J. Kennett et al., *Nature Communications* 8:14115 (2017). DOI: 10.1038/ncomms14115. Open access.

**What this paper establishes about Pueblo Bonito Room 33:**

The abstract states directly:
> "We show that nine individuals buried in an elite crypt at Pueblo Bonito, the largest structure in the canyon, have identical mitochondrial genomes. Analyses of nuclear genome data from six samples with the highest DNA preservation demonstrate mother–daughter and grandmother–grandson relationships, evidence for a multigenerational matrilineal descent group. Together, these results demonstrate the persistence of an elite matriline in Chaco for ~330 years."

On the burial assemblage of the founding individual (Burial 14):
> "His body was placed in the centre of the burial crypt on a prepared floor of sand and wood ashes and adorned with thousands of turquoise (n>11,200) and shell (n>3,300) beads and pendants, originally part of necklaces, anklets and bracelets. The abundance of turquoise alone makes this the richest burial known in the North American Southwest. Also placed on the right side of the individual were several other unusual offerings including multiple abalone (Haliotis spp.) shells from the Pacific Coast and a conch shell trumpet."

On the broader assemblage in Room 33:
> "Nearby were caches of flutes in the northeastern corner and carved [ritual objects]." The room was "purposely constructed as a crypt for a high-status member of this nascent community."

**Evaluative implications for this corpus:**

Room 33 is the canonical reference for what a high-investment, elite depositional context looks like in the prehispanic Southwest. Key criteria established by this paper:

- **Assemblage scale as social signal**: 11,200+ turquoise pieces, 3,300+ shell ornaments in a single burial establishes a quantitative benchmark. Objects from this assemblage are not evaluated individually — they are read as a system.
- **Multi-material composition as status indicator**: turquoise + Gulf of California shell + Pacific Coast abalone + conch trumpet + wooden ritual objects. The combination of non-local materials from multiple distant sources is the evidence of elite access to exchange networks.
- **Depositional sequence as evidence**: the prepared sand and wood-ash floor, the plank-covered second interment, the sequential addition of later burials — these stratigraphic details are analytically meaningful. Disturbed or undocumented context destroys this evidence.
- **aDNA as interpretive tool**: this paper demonstrates that ancient DNA analysis can resolve long-standing debates about social organization (kinship-based vs. achievement-based leadership) that material analysis alone could not. Compositional and genomic analysis now routinely inform interpretations that once relied on typology and context alone.

## Vivian, Dodgen, and Hartmann — *Wooden Ritual Artifacts from Chaco Canyon, New Mexico: The Chetro Ketl Collection*
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: R. Gwinn Vivian, Dulce N. Dodgen, and Gayle Harrison Hartmann, *Anthropological Papers of the University of Arizona No. 32* (1978; open-access reissue 2022). PDF: https://open.uapress.arizona.edu/read/wooden-ritual-artifacts-from-chaco-canyon-new-mexico-the-chetro-ketl-collection

**On the collection and its limits:**

The authors are explicit about provenience constraints:
> "While the material significantly expands our perception of the structural and decorative attributes of this Chacoan art form, the condition of the artifacts and their contextual data place limitations on the functional interpretation of the collection."
> "Our goals were limited by the artifact provenience data... the collection is incomplete and... the position of the items in the room does not reflect placement in a ritual context."
> "Room 93 represents a unit out of context in terms of the excavation of Chetro Ketl... the interpretation of the use of Room 93 in terms of the prehistory of Chetro Ketl and especially the northern room block is severely limited."

**Formal categories established for carved/painted wood:**

The authors organize the collection into three morphological categories:
- **Flat carved forms**: slats, plaques, discs, ovates, "arrows" — thin flat pieces, painted on one or both surfaces
- **Round carved forms**: zoomorphic effigies (primarily birds, possibly serpents), horns, wands, ferrule sticks
- **Miscellaneous wooden and vegetal objects**: cordage, worked sticks, gourd rind discs, corn husk packets

**Material and pigment vocabulary (from Appendix B technical analysis):**

Wood species identified: cottonwood (primary), juniper, willow, alder, pine. Species selection was deliberate — cottonwood was the standard medium for ritual carved wood across multiple Southwest traditions.

Pigments documented on the collection: turquoise green, black, red, white, yellow, orange, carmine, deep carmine, blue. Pigment application method: ground coat of a single color covering the full surface, with design elements painted over in contrasting colors.

The characteristic Chacoan painted-wood color palette: turquoise green and dead black, supplemented by white, dark red, and brown — documented across multiple Chacoan sites (Room 93 Chetro Ketl, Room 32 Pueblo Bonito, Tseh So village).

**On the function category:**

The authors reject the "intact altar" interpretation in favor of stored ritual paraphernalia:
> "We suggest storage of ritual artifacts as an alternative to the intact altar hypothesis. Sacrosanct objects are often placed in back rooms of Pueblo villages when not in use... Storage is usually temporary unless the items are no longer used, which may occur as a result of the loss of ceremonial knowledge or the death of the last person familiar with the ritual."

This matters for analysis: the collection is in secondary depositional context (storage/removal from primary ritual context), not primary (active altar use). Evidence of primary context — objects assembled and arranged for ritual use — would look different.

**Evaluative criteria for carved/painted wood objects:**

Drawing from the comparative analysis across Chacoan and Mogollon sites:

*Form criteria*: completeness of zoomorphic form (whether anatomical features — wings, tail, beak, legs — are differentiated); regularity of carved reduction (absence of tool chatter marks, splits, unintended gouging); reconstruction potential (how many fragments rejoin to form a whole object).

*Surface treatment criteria*: coverage of ground coat (even vs. mottled); color range and number of colors employed; design-element execution (interlocking frets, serrated edges, geometric fields, biomorphic outlines); painting on both surfaces vs. one surface (both surfaces = higher investment); presence of perforations for suspension or attachment (indicates composite ritual use, not standalone object).

*Material criteria*: species identification (cottonwood is the conventional ritual medium; departures signal either non-local production or different functional category); evidence of deliberate species selection vs. expedient use of available wood.

*Preservation criteria*: paint retention vs. paint loss; structural integrity vs. fragmentation; evidence of repair or deliberate re-use; evidence of intentional destruction (breaks consistent with vandalism vs. post-depositional damage).

**The canonical reference assemblage:**

The Chetro Ketl collection (Room 93) is the largest and best-preserved carved/painted wood assemblage from the Ancestral Puebloan/Chaco tradition. The authors state:
> "The painted and carved wood from Chetro Ketl is the largest and best preserved collection of its type yet reported from the Anasazi culture area."

Secondary reference assemblages for comparison: Room 32 Pueblo Bonito (design board + prayerstick cache, Pepper 1920); Tseh So village (cottonwood effigy forms, turquoise green and black palette); Mogollon cave sites (Tularosa Cave, Cordova Cave — Martin et al. 1952; U-Bar Cave — Lambert and Ambler 1961).

## Harry J. Shafer — "Some Thoughts about Mimbres Pottery and Mortuary Customs"
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: Harry J. Shafer, "Some Thoughts about Mimbres Pottery and Mortuary Customs," in *Making "Meaning": Precolumbian Archaeology, Art History, and the Legacy of Terence Grieder*, University of Houston Libraries Pressbooks (2022). Open access, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. URL: https://uh.pressbooks.pub/precolumbian/chapter/mimbres-pottery-and-mortuary-customs/
Note: Full text accessed in prior session; verbatim passages extracted at that time.

**On Mimbres ceramic technology:**

> "The exquisitely painted Mimbres pottery is a white-slipped brownware. It is technologically made of generally poor-quality clays using the coil-and-scrape method of production."

This establishes the baseline: Mimbres Classic Black-on-White is white-slipped brownware, coil-and-scrape construction. The term "generally poor-quality clays" is Shafer's — it contextualizes the investment as being in surface treatment and painted decoration, not in clay body refinement.

**On the style sequence:**

> "Style I is distinguished by bold lines with wavy cross-hatched patterns."
> "Style II is identified by geometric and naturalistic motifs outlined with bold lines with fine-line cross-hatching."
> "In Style III, hatched motifs are all fine line."

The three-style sequence — I (bold geometric), II (bold outline with fine-line infill), III (all fine line) — is the standard typological framework. These distinctions are observable from photographs in most cases. Style III is the Classic period (roughly 1000–1130 CE); Styles I and II precede it.

**On domestic use before burial:**

> "They were made to be used in everyday functions, such as cooking, serving, and storage, as shown by the use-wear exhibited on the interior of the bowls."

This is the primary source statement for the domestic use evidence. Use-wear on the interior is the observable marker. The burial context is an endpoint, not the original function. This directly supports the existing [PARAPHRASE] entry for Shafer's NAN Ranch book on the same point.

**On production geography and craft organization:**

> "Recent NAA studies have shown that most of the pottery was produced in the upper elevations of the Mimbres region by a limited number of cottage-level craft specialists."

The NAA studies referenced are Creel and Speakman (2018). "Cottage-level craft specialists" is the authors' term — not household production by every family, not full-time workshop production, but a limited number of part-time specialists producing for a broader community or region. This modifies the earlier [PARAPHRASE] entries on craft specialization (Spielmann) for the Mimbres case specifically.

**On kill holes:**

Shafer describes kill hole methods as "perforating near the bottom. The perforations were usually done by puncturing the bowl with a pointed stone from the exterior or interior, drilled, or smashed."

Three observable methods: (1) single-point punch from exterior, (2) single-point punch from interior, (3) smashing through. All three produce different scar profiles. Drill perforations are also documented. Location: near the bottom, not the center, not the rim. This detail matters for distinguishing intentional kill holes from accidental damage.

**Evaluative implications for this corpus:**

- **Technology marker**: white-slipped brownware + coil-and-scrape = Mimbres attribution. Both features are observable.
- **Style placement**: use the three-style sequence (I/II/III) for typological placement. Style III = Classic period = highest symbolic investment.
- **Use-wear as evidence**: interior abrasion = domestic use before burial. Absence of use-wear = possible production-for-burial.
- **Kill hole identification**: look for location (near base), method scar (punch vs. drill vs. smash), and distinguish from post-depositional damage.
- **Production context**: "limited number of cottage-level craft specialists" — avoid framing Mimbres vessels as either anonymous household products or elite atelier products. The term to use is cottage-level specialist.

## Harry J. Shafer — *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* — Chapter 10: Mimbres Pottery
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: Harry J. Shafer, *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2003), Chapter 10 "Mimbres Pottery." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbresarchaelog0000shaf

**On overall quality — a frank assessment:**

> "Their pots are often lopsided and rarely symmetrical; some are thick, and others are thin."
> "the ancient Mimbreños were not the best ancient potters in the Southwest"

These are Shafer's own words from the NAN Ranch monograph. He is making a technical quality claim about the clay body and vessel forming — not about the painting. The contrast he sets up is explicit:
> "Technologically speaking, Mimbres brownwares are generally poorly made, with much softer fabrics than are customarily found in the Cibola area to the north. But aesthetically speaking, Mimbres pottery had no contemporary rivals."

This is the key evaluative split: technical production quality (clay, forming, fabric hardness) is below regional norms; painted decoration quality is above any contemporaneous tradition. Analysis should apply these as separate axes.

**On the style sequence (NAN Ranch evidence):**

Style I (~AD 800–900):
> "bold line designs executed in opposed or four-quadrant fields" — rim to rim extent; wavy lines as filler; hachure used

Style II (~AD 850–1010):
> "use of fine lines as hachure in elements bordered by bold lines" — framing bands begin in late Style II

Style III (Classic period, ~AD 1000–1130+):
> fine-line hachure bordered by fine lines; rim bands; naturalistic motifs fully developed

NAN Ranch chronological detail — middle Style III (~AD 1050–1110):
> "height of production"

On Mimbres Polychrome within Style III:
> "Mimbres Polychrome vessels are among the most exquisitely painted examples of Style III"
> "rare, <1% of all painted ware"

**On prestige and social function:**

> "I firmly believe the production of Mimbres painted pottery was to reinforce the system of values and ceremonies sustaining the corporate networks that operated the irrigation systems"
> "Style II and III pottery can be considered prestige items to display success and power"

**On domestic use:**

> "Extensive use wear on over 80 percent of mortuary vessels clearly documents their domestic uses"

This is the NAN Ranch-specific figure (80%+) for the domestic use claim; the open-access essay (above) states the same principle. Both sources converge.

**Evaluative implications:**

- Do not conflate technical quality (clay body, forming symmetry, fabric hardness) with aesthetic quality (painted surface execution). These are independent axes in Shafer's analysis.
- Polychrome is the rarest, highest-investment variant of Style III. A correct polychrome identification with good surface preservation is a significant finding.
- Style II/III distinction is observable from photograph: the key diagnostic is whether hachure is bordered by bold lines (Style II) or by fine lines (Style III).
- 80%+ domestic use-wear rate at NAN Ranch means absence of use-wear is notable and worth flagging in analysis.

## Harry J. Shafer — *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* — Chapter 11: Technologies and Crafts
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: Harry J. Shafer, *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2003), Chapter 11 "Technologies and Crafts." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbresarchaelog0000shaf

**On stone tool technology:**

Expedient tools dominate: andesite/basalt and rhyolite used for informal flake tools. Obsidian is the prestige/distance-traded material:
- Sources documented at NAN Ranch: Mule Creek (eastern Arizona), Ewe Canyon, Antelope Wells — all 120–150 km distant
- Obsidian = non-local exchange participation; andesite/rhyolite = local expedient production

Arrow point types at NAN Ranch (Classic period markers):
- **Hinton**: parallel side notches, concave base
- **Swarts**: parallel side notches, convex base
- **Cosgrove**: parallel side notches, multiple notches on one edge

All three are Mimbres Classic diagnostic point types.

**On metate evolution as agricultural intensification evidence:**

Oval basin → trough open one end → trough open both ends

This sequence correlates directly with increasing maize processing intensity. The through-trough (open both ends) is the most efficient form and dominates at Classic Mimbres sites. Presence of oval basin metates signals either early occupation or non-Classic component.

**On greenstone axes as prestige items:**

> "These artifacts were prestige items apparently brought to the site in finished form"

Source material: uralitized diabase, quarry located approximately 17 km northwest of NAN Ranch. Distribution: found on floors of virtually every residential unit at NAN Ranch.

Interpretive implication: near-universal residential distribution does not equal low status — it suggests a prestige item that was widely accessible within the community but not locally produced.

**On fiber production:**

Basketry and textiles documented at NAN Ranch — techniques: twinning, coiling, plaiting. Shafer describes this as the "largest extant collection of fiber artifacts from any Classic Mimbres site" — making NAN Ranch the reference assemblage for Mimbres fiber.

**On shell ornament assemblage:**

Shell species count at NAN Ranch (from Shafer's table):
- Glycymeris: 361 specimens
- Nassarius: 182
- Pecten: 137
- Haliotis (abalone): 81
- Spondylus: 74
- Olivella: 34
- Conus: 5
- coral: 2
- Strombus: 2

> "adornment jewelry may be marking certain individuals as members of a specific kin group or sodality"

Distribution pattern at NAN Ranch: shell bracelet and bead adornment restricted to males.

**On copper bells (crotals):**

3 specimens total at NAN Ranch. Most significant context: one found with Burial 188, an adult male, worn on the right wrist. Copper crotals = Mesoamerican-linked prestige item; rare at any Mimbres site.

**Evaluative implications:**

- Obsidian = non-local exchange participation, 120–150 km minimum distance. Presence is analytically significant; source attribution requires compositional analysis.
- Spondylus presence in shell assemblages = long-distance Pacific or Gulf exchange network; always analytically significant regardless of context.
- Greenstone ax = prestige indicator even at NAN Ranch where they appear in most rooms — the "ubiquitous prestige item" pattern.
- Copper crotal = highest-tier Mimbres prestige marker; Mesoamerican-linked; rare at any Mimbres site.
- Shell bracelet/bead adornment restricted to males at NAN Ranch — gender-specific ornament pattern.

## Harry J. Shafer — *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* — Chapter 8: Mortuary Customs
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: Harry J. Shafer, *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2003), Chapter 8 "Mortuary Customs." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbresarchaelog0000shaf

**On use-wear and mortuary selection:**

> "Over 75 percent of the pottery vessels used in burials show distinct signs of domestic use (sooting, chipping, scratching, or abrasive wear)"

The specific use-wear markers Shafer names: sooting, chipping, scratching, abrasive wear. All four are observable on exterior and interior surfaces. This is the primary source statement — 75%+ figure from the full NAN Ranch burial assemblage.

**On kill hole timing — when the practice began:**

> "placing a 'killed' bowl over the head of the deceased of all age groups did not begin until the Late Three Circle phase"

This establishes a chronological constraint: kill hole practice = Classic period marker. Pre-Classic Mimbres mortuary assemblages lack kill holes. Age range at NAN Ranch: all age groups, from infant to elder.

**On burial location (intramural transition):**

- Extramural burial = Late Pithouse period
- Intramural (under house floors) = Classic period

The intramural practice is the defining mortuary pattern of Classic Mimbres. Shafer's NAN Ranch data documents this transition at a single site.

**On body position:**

NAN Ranch statistics: flexed (64.84%) and semiflexed (27.47%) are the dominant positions — together accounting for 92%+ of interments. Extended burial is rare and potentially anomalous.

**On grave markers:**

Rock cairns over graves = Classic period trait at NAN Ranch. Absence of cairn ≠ absence of burial.

**On the absence of ranked burials:**

> "Were there 'big men' in Mimbres society? If so, none were found at the NAN Ranch Ruin"

Shafer explicitly finds no evidence for vertical ranking at NAN Ranch in burial assemblage wealth. This matters for interpretation: wealth accumulation in Mimbres burials cannot be read as simple rank marker the way it can at Pueblo Bonito.

**On specific notable burials:**

Burial 86 (adult female, Room 14): found with pottery-making kit — interpreted by Shafer as likely a potter. This is the primary evidence for individual production identity in the NAN Ranch assemblage.

Burial 127 (adult male in prepared crypt): tubular cloudblower pipe + quartz crystal + turtle plastron + Olivella beads + projectile points. Shafer interprets this as a possible shaman kit. The combination of objects — not any single item — is the interpretive basis.

Burial 175: primary cremation with evidence of rack construction and public ceremony — the only cremation with this level of elaboration at NAN Ranch.

**Evaluative implications:**

- Use-wear on 75%+ of NAN Ranch mortuary vessels = strong baseline for "domestic use before burial" interpretation. Absence of use-wear on a vessel (sooting, interior abrasion) is analytically notable.
- Kill hole = Classic period (post-Late Three Circle). Presence dates the burial context. Absence does not date it.
- Rock cairn marker = additional dating signal for Classic period burial.
- Flexed/semiflexed posture = expected; extended = anomalous.
- Burial assemblage wealth at Mimbres sites does not reliably indicate rank — NAN Ranch shows no vertical ranking even in the most elaborate burials.
- Combination of objects (pipe + crystal + plastron) rather than any single object is the basis for functional interpretation (shaman kit, specialist, high-status kin).

## J.J. Brody — *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (revised ed. 2004) — Chapter 8: Representational Paintings
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: J.J. Brody, *Mimbres Painted Pottery*, revised edition (Santa Fe: School of American Research Press / Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2004), Chapter 8 "Representational Paintings." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbrespaintedpo0000brod_a5u9

**On the "representational" vs. "geometric" distinction:**

Brody explicitly rejects a hard binary:
> "representational" and "geometric" are not mutually exclusive; all images are abstractions

This is analytically important: even "geometric" Mimbres paintings are not pure abstraction, and even "representational" ones operate through formal conventions rather than illusionism.

**On corpus-level statistics (Table 2):**

From Brody's sample of 733 published and unpublished images:
- 64% single figure
- 15% two figures, non-narrative
- 9% narrative with humans
- 7% narrative without humans

Single-figure compositions are the statistical norm, not narrative multi-figure scenes. Multi-figure narrative is the minority mode — but the most culturally dense one.

**On single-figure composition conventions:**

Figure isolated against blank background; usually shown in profile, full-face, or from above; static posture. The blank ground — no landscape, no ground line in most cases — is a compositional choice, not a limitation.

**On the core creative principles:**

> "Interchangeability, transformation, and being and becoming are the core creative principles of Mimbres art"

This is Brody's highest-level interpretive claim about what Mimbres figuration is doing. Transformation between states (human/animal, alive/dead, one species/another) is not a failure of naturalistic representation — it is the subject.

**On polar orientation:**

Figures tilted on axes radiating from the center of the bowl, creating a perspective illusion relative to the vessel's round-bottomed form. This is a systematic compositional feature, not individual variation.

**On descriptive ambiguity as artistic strategy:**

> "Rather than holding representational illusionism or illustration of the natural world as their goal, it would seem that Mimbres artists, in their life-form paintings, relished the invention of descriptive ambiguities and visual puns"

This quote directly addresses the question of artistic intent. The ambiguity is not failure — it is the method.

**On what makes a figurative painting successful:**

> "Most successful figurative paintings: narratives using framing line as ground line OR polar one-point perspective"

Two compositional success modes: (1) the framing line used as a ground line on which figures stand or act; (2) polar orientation with figures radiating from the center.

**On iconographic identification criteria:**

For animal figures: full-face view (unusual — most animals are profile), position relative to other figures, associated objects or activity. Brody notes that some identifications are viewer imagination, not authorial intent.
> "The identification of an image with a natural form is an act of imagination on the part of the viewer that may bear no relation to what the artist had in mind."

**Evaluative implications:**

- Use Brody's two-axis model: geometric vs. representational is not a binary; assess degree of figuration on a spectrum.
- Single-figure/non-narrative = 79% of corpus; do not over-read narrative content into what may be a static single-figure composition.
- Descriptive ambiguity (dual readings, transformation figures, composite beings) is a feature, not a defect.
- Framing line as ground line = compositional sophistication marker; polar orientation = systematic compositional structure.
- "Interchangeability, transformation, being and becoming" — use these terms when the image shows hybrid or transformation figures; they are Brody's analytic vocabulary for this phenomenon.

## J.J. Brody — *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (revised ed. 2004) — Chapter 7: Form and Structure
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: J.J. Brody, *Mimbres Painted Pottery*, revised edition (Santa Fe: School of American Research Press / Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2004), Chapter 7 "Form and Structure." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbrespaintedpo0000brod_a5u9

**On vessel form as pictorial ground:**

> "painting was applied to the surface of a vessel as a sort of skin"

The bowl interior is not a flat canvas — it is a hemispherical concave surface approximately 9 inches in diameter and 4–5 inches deep, round-bottomed. The painting system was developed specifically for this form.

**On systematic exclusions:**

> "no paint textures other than shiny surface; no more than two colors (except polychrome exceptions)"
> "two-dimensional draftsmanship avoided shading or other devices that might create illusions of three-dimensional solidity"

These are the formal rules of the Classic Mimbres painting system. Shading, three-dimensional illusionism, and color beyond two (or three in polychrome) are ruled out by convention. Their absence is not a limitation — it is the system.

**On structural organization:**

Basic pictorial structures: quartered patterns (most common), radial divisions, reserved center void. Linear elements: triangles (most common geometric element), circles, diamonds, rhomboids, squares, crosses, spirals.

Motif type distinction:
- **Structural motifs**: dominant, center-oriented, drive the overall composition
- **Non-structural motifs (fillers)**: subsidiary elements that fill zones defined by structural motifs

**On line quality as a criterion:**

> "Line quality was remarkably consistent throughout the entire Classic Mimbres period and throughout the Mimbres region"

Lines are even-sided and narrow. Long lines were made as connected dashes fused by skilled painters. This consistency is a system property, not individual variation.

> "Indifferent draftsmanship voided visual success no matter how fertile the imagination. Superb draftsmen who followed the rules could hardly go wrong."

This is Brody's clearest statement of the craft-quality criterion. Line control is the technical prerequisite; design invention operates within that constraint.

**On positive-negative ambiguity:**

> "strikingly ambiguous positive-negative patterns are among the most active and effective of all Mimbres geometric paintings"

The system depends on the ability to read a design as either dark-on-light or light-on-dark simultaneously. This is not accidental — it is a property that the most skilled Mimbres painters developed deliberately.

> "The Mimbres geometric painting system often depended on the use of active, well-defined, overall motif that can be read as either positive or negative images. Alternatively, and often simultaneously, it also called for subdividing space into ever smaller units that could be visually reintegrated into a single, dominating, overall pattern. Ambiguity, motion, and tension were built into the system and were reinforced by specific methodologies."

**On color masking:**

Tonal variation created "color" effects beyond the two-color system: hatching creates gray tones; burnished paint creates subtle surface variation. Half or more of the picture space was often covered by paint, but activity and contrast of shape kept pictures from being somber.

**On the role of tradition:**

> "The limitations and constraints of that tradition were a guarantee of consistency, and the superficial visual conservatism that seems to be so alien to the creative process was, instead, an essential protective device that made pictorial invention possible."
> "Rather than inhibiting the artists, tradition provided them with a framework within which each one could build with confidence to the full limits of individual creative ability."

**Evaluative implications:**

- The evaluation axis is not "freedom of expression" but "mastery within a constraining system." Assess line control, positive-negative ambiguity, structural vs. non-structural motif integration.
- Quartered composition is the baseline; more complex radial divisions or reserved center voids signal compositional sophistication.
- Two-color constraint is the norm; polychrome is an exceptional departure.
- "Indifferent draftsmanship" vs. "superb draftsmen who followed the rules" is Brody's language for quality assessment. Use it.
- Positive-negative ambiguity — the capacity of the design to be read in two ways simultaneously — is the hallmark of the most effective geometric paintings.

## J.J. Brody — *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (revised ed. 2004) — Chapter 6: The Potters and Their Craft
[PRIMARY SOURCE EXTRACT]
Source: J.J. Brody, *Mimbres Painted Pottery*, revised edition (Santa Fe: School of American Research Press / Albuquerque: University of New Mexico Press, 2004), Chapter 6 "The Potters and Their Craft." Archive.org loan. URL: https://archive.org/details/mimbrespaintedpo0000brod_a5u9

**On craft specialization — the definitive statement:**

> "no evidence suggests that anything approaching full-time craft specialization or professionalism existed. Still, the Mimbres unquestionably made fine art without the luxury of having full-time artists."

Brody's position on specialization is explicit: Mimbres painting is not the product of full-time specialist workshops. It is part-time community-embedded production that achieved exceptional results through tradition and training — not through professional specialization.

> "Mimbres Classic era pottery painting gives the impression of being so consistently high in quality that something like specialization must have developed. This impression is misleading, for only the finest examples are usually published or seen, and examination of several thousand vessels suggests a much wider range in quality than is generally on view in museum exhibits, private collections, and publications."

This is a methodological warning: published and collected Mimbres pottery is a highly skewed sample biased toward high-quality examples. The full range of quality — including children's work, unskilled adult paintings, and regional substyle variation — is visible only in large controlled excavation assemblages.

**On painter identity and gender:**

> "the usual high quality of Mimbres narrative paintings on pottery demonstrates that no matter who painted them, the artists were generally well trained, skilled, creative, and experienced"

> "Some poorly made vessels that are also poorly painted seem to be entirely the work of children or other novices"

> "Other paintings, almost always narrative scenes that are decidedly clumsy in their execution but that appear on well-made vessels, suggest the work of an iconographically knowledgeable adult other than the potter, possibly a male but in any event relatively unskilled as a pottery painter"

Brody distinguishes three production modes from the evidence:
1. Skilled women painters — the norm; responsible for most high-quality work
2. Children/novice painters — observable by both poor vessel forming and poor painting on the same object
3. Iconographically knowledgeable but technically unskilled adult painters — narrative scenes with correct iconographic content but poor line control; possibly male contributors

**On tradition vs. innovation:**

> "conscious striving for radical innovations or highly individualistic personal styles would have been unthinkable"
> "The Mimbres aesthetic evolved slowly and with a seemingly inevitable logic that permitted innovations only within strictly understood limits. The most radical innovation was the introduction of representational subjects, but the forms these took evolved in accordance with the rules."

> "exceptional Mimbres painters produced exceptional paintings, but the quality of their work was always related to group standards or social ideals. The restrictive traditions of the group and its high expectations ensured a predominantly high level of performance that served as the base for the occasional production of a memorable picture."

**On the manufacturing sequence (chaîne opératoire):**

Clay preparation:
- Low-firing, redeposited clays from arroyo banks and plaza areas
- Soaked in water for weeks or months; biological action improves plasticity
- Kneaded in metate to consistent texture; air pockets removed
- Temper: sand for cooking/storage jars; volcanic stone (rhyolite or volcanic ash) for bowls; ~1/2 inch diameter coils, 6–8 inches long

Coil construction:
- Support mold: bottom of a broken pottery bowl or basket base
- Coils added outward and upward; each welded and smoothed during construction
- Wall thickness after scraping: approximately 3/16 inch
> "If a potter failed to weld and smooth the coils while they were plastic, not only would the pot be weak, but air bubbles in the clay might expand and explode when heated, destroying the vessel itself during firing"

Surface preparation:
- Slipping: white volcanic ash-derived clay, interior only in Mimbres bowls (exterior slip = unusual, possibly outlying area production)
- Applied as thin wash, absorbed instantly; several thin layers preferred over one thick layer
- Polishing: moistened smooth stone; burnishing marks at right angles or diagonal to rim
> "The ideal seems to have been a satin-smooth, somewhat reflective, even-toned, pure white surface, but as a rule, Mimbres slip clays tend to be somewhat gray and not very reflective."

Painting:
- Pigment: iron-rich, red, slip-quality clay ground to fine powder; mixed with water; possible Rocky Mountain beeweed (Cleome serrulata) as binder
- Brush: narrow-leaf yucca, split to desired line thickness; 3–7 inches long; 1–3 inches chewed soft for paint application; remainder = handle
> "A yucca brush is a highly specialized tool, so limp when wet that if it is held and used as if it were either stiff or flexible, it makes an uncontrollable, ragged line. But once a person learns to control this unresponsive, blunt-ended, square-sided instrument, it is ideal for making straight, even-edged lines on a curved, concave, or convex surface."
> "Paint had to be applied with a sure stroke and precise knowledge of where each brush mark was supposed to go and what it was supposed to do."

Error handling:
- Errors could not be truly erased — paint absorbed into slip; only scraping removes both
- Most errors were simply ignored or overpainted
- Overpainted lines and patch marks are archaeological evidence of individual painting events

Firing:
- Bonfire kiln; 15–20 vessels per load; approximately 800 degrees C; 90-minute burn
- Warm, still, dry days required
- Fire clouds (black marks on exterior from burning fuel contact) are "virtually ubiquitous" on Mimbres bowls — deliberately tolerated, not prevented
> "It seems clear that Mimbres potters deliberately courted certain kinds of accidents and imperfections — an unexpected aspect of their aesthetic and a counterbalance to the perfection of patterning that they usually sought and often achieved in their paintings."

**On firing atmosphere and color as aesthetic choice:**

> "so many are partly black and partly red or brown that it seems reasonable to conclude that in some instances, if not most, the effect was desired and deliberate rather than a consequence of poor firing control"

> "I interpret their consistent failure to do so [build windscreens] as the exercise of a deliberate aesthetic choice: they courted accidents by controlling firing conditions to encourage the creation of painted vessels with colors ranging from black to red on the same surface."

Firing atmosphere mechanics:
- Reduction atmosphere (low oxygen) → black/gray paint, gray-white slip
- Oxidizing atmosphere → red/brown paint, gray-cream slip
- Many Classic Mimbres paintings are deliberately mixed black-and-red — a controlled accident

**On regional substyles:**

> "Mimbres pottery painters in some upper Gila villages seem to have used grayer slips that were less well polished than those in the Mimbres Valley and they painted much thinner bottom framing lines on their bowl interiors than was customary elsewhere."
> "Thin or no framing lines seem characteristic also of paintings made farther east, along the drainage of the Rio Grande."

Neutron activation analysis (NAA) has confirmed local production in the places tested:
> "Mimbres painted pottery was generally made at the place where it was found"

This regional production evidence means vessel stylistic features can tentatively identify production locality (Mimbres Valley vs. upper Gila vs. Rio Grande drainage) even without compositional analysis.

**On the social embedding of production:**

> "The technical and social constraints of the craft provided no more than the matrix or field needed to make the work intelligible to its users, who were ordinarily members of the potter's own small community."

> "The end products had important communicative and aesthetic capabilities, and the care taken by Mimbres potters in making and decorating their vessels attests to the aesthetic and symbolic values given to the art by that community."

**Evaluative implications:**

- Quality range is wide; the published/collected sample is biased toward high quality. Do not assume a photographed vessel represents median Mimbres skill.
- Three production modes observable: (1) skilled adult — controlled line, correct style conventions; (2) child/novice — poor forming AND poor painting on same vessel; (3) skilled forming + unskilled narrative painting — iconographic knowledge without technical line control.
- Yucca brush mechanics explain the characteristic Mimbres line: even-sided, narrow, consistent in width — the result of a limp brush drawn at controlled speed. Ragged or variable-width lines signal poor brush control, not different brush type.
- Fire clouds on exterior = expected; their absence is unusual, not their presence.
- Mixed black-and-red firing effect = possible deliberate aesthetic choice, not necessarily firing failure.
- Exterior slip = possible regional marker (upper Gila or outlying production).
- Framing line thickness is a regional substyle marker: thin or absent framing lines = possibly upper Gila or Rio Grande area production.
