## Primary Scholars

### J.J. Brody
Art historian and archaeologist. The most prolific writer on Mimbres ceramic imagery.
Primary work: *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (1977, revised 2004, School of American Research Press).

Brody approached Mimbres pottery as an art historical problem — treating the vessels as visual objects with iconographic programs subject to formal and comparative analysis. He documented the full range of figurative and geometric imagery, established the sequence of stylistic development, and argued that the imagery reflects a coherent symbolic system rather than random decoration. His framework is the dominant reference point in the field even for scholars who challenge his conclusions.

Key argument: The figurative program of Classic Mimbres pottery is not incidental decoration but a systematic visual language. Individual images need to be understood within the full corpus of Mimbres imagery before their meaning can be assessed.

### Michelle Hegmon
Archaeologist specializing in social organization and ceramic variability. Primary work: "Social Integration and System Stress: Lessons from the Mimbres" (in various edited volumes); co-author of multiple peer-reviewed studies on Mimbres social complexity.

Hegmon applies a social-theory framework rather than an art-historical one. She treats ceramic production and distribution as evidence of social organization — who made pots, for whom, under what social arrangements, and what that tells us about hierarchy, specialization, and community structure. She challenges readings that emphasize individual artistic expression, arguing that pottery production was embedded in social practice.

Key argument: Variability in Mimbres pottery is not primarily a function of individual artistic choice but of social and organizational factors — production context, distribution networks, and community identity.

### Harry Shafer
Archaeologist. Director of excavations at NAN Ranch Ruin, one of the most systematically documented Mimbres sites. Primary work: *Mimbres Archaeology at the NAN Ranch Ruin* (2003, University of New Mexico Press).

Shafer's contribution is stratigraphic rigor and production analysis. NAN Ranch provided detailed contextual data — where vessels were made, how production changed over time, the relationship between domestic pottery use and burial pottery. He demonstrated that most Classic Mimbres bowls were used before burial, not made specifically as mortuary goods.

Key argument: Mimbres pottery was part of everyday domestic life. The burial context is an endpoint, not the original purpose. Wear patterns and production sequence data support this reading.

### Steven LeBlanc
Archaeologist specializing in prehistoric warfare and demography. Primary work: *Prehistoric Warfare in the American Southwest* (1999, University of Utah Press); earlier excavations at Mimbres sites.

LeBlanc brought demographic and conflict frameworks to Mimbres analysis, arguing that the Classic Mimbres period involved significant population pressure and inter-community conflict. This affects interpretation of site organization, defensive architecture, and the abrupt abandonment of Mimbres settlements around 1130–1150 CE.

Key argument: The end of Classic Mimbres culture was not a gradual transformation but a collapse driven by demographic stress and warfare, not simply environmental change or voluntary dispersal.

### Barbara Moulard
Art historian and curator. Focuses on narrative sequences in Mimbres figurative imagery, arguing that individual images read as single frames within larger mythological or ritual narratives.

Key argument: Mimbres figurative images are not standalone symbols but episodes — analogous to panels in a narrative sequence. Their meaning is partly reconstructable through comparison with other vessels depicting related figures or events.

### Darrell Creel
Archaeologist. Field excavations at multiple Mimbres sites; contributed to regional chronology and transition analysis from Pithouse to Pueblo period. Useful on ceramic typology and stratigraphic sequence.

### Margaret Nelson
Archaeologist specializing in abandonment processes in the Southwest. Relevant to interpreting the end of Classic Mimbres and what the ceramic and architectural record tells us about how communities dissolved.

## Analytical Frameworks in Play

### Iconographic analysis (Brody tradition)
Treats ceramic imagery as a visual program to be decoded through formal description, comparative analysis across the corpus, and cross-cultural parallels. Assumes the imagery has meaning recoverable through systematic analysis. Contested by processual archaeologists who see it as over-interpreting.

### Technological style / chaîne opératoire
Studies production sequence as social information — the choices made in selecting clay, temper, forming, surface treatment, and firing are not purely technical but encode social identity, learned practice, and cultural affiliation. Useful for distinguishing Mimbres-tradition pottery from trade wares or imitations.

### Agency and social identity
Asks what role pottery played in constructing and expressing social positions — gender, craft specialization, community membership, ritual role. Pushes against frameworks that treat pots as passive reflections of culture.

### Ceramic ecology / material analysis
Petrographic and chemical analysis (INAA — Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis) of clay, temper, and slip to establish production loci and trace exchange networks. Determines whether a vessel was locally produced or obtained through trade.

## Key Unresolved Debates

1. **Meaning of figurative imagery** — Art-historical readings (Brody, Moulard) see a coherent symbolic system; processual critics see over-interpretation without ethnographic analogy. No consensus.

2. **Mortuary specificity** — Were decorated bowls made for burial or used in life? Shafer's NAN Ranch data supports in-life use; some vessels show no wear suggesting mortuary production. Both appear to be true for different vessels.

3. **Craft specialization** — Was pottery production household-based or specialized? Evidence supports household production with emergent specialization toward the end of Classic period.

4. **Kill hole purpose** — "Killing" the vessel to release its spirit (most common interpretation), or practical reasons (prevent reuse of burial goods, or ritual completion of the vessel's life). Not resolved.

5. **Abandonment cause** — Warfare/demographic collapse (LeBlanc), environmental degradation, reorganization into new community forms, or Salado/Hohokam interaction? Probably a combination.
