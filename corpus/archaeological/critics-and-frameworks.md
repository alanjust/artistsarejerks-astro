## Foundational Synthetic Works

### Linda Cordell — *Archaeology of the Southwest* (1997, 2nd ed.)
The standard synthetic overview of Southwestern prehistory. Covers all major traditions from Paleoindian through historic period, with thorough treatment of ceramic, architectural, and material culture evidence across regions. Essential reference for placing any Southwest artifact in regional and temporal context. Cordell's strength is synthesizing across traditions without forcing uniformity — she maintains the distinctiveness of Hohokam, Mogollon, and Ancestral Puebloan trajectories while identifying shared processes.

Key argument: The Southwest is not a uniform culture area but a mosaic of interacting regional traditions. Material culture variability reflects both local adaptation and participation in broader interaction networks.

### Polly Schaafsma
Rock art specialist and Southwest iconographer. Primary works: *Indian Rock Art of the Southwest* (1980); *Warrior, Shield, and Star* (2000); various edited volumes on Pueblo IV imagery. Schaafsma documents the long-term continuity of Southwest iconographic traditions across material categories — the same symbols appear in ceramics, rock art, murals, and portable objects across centuries. Critical for any analysis of Southwest iconographic programs because she establishes which motifs are pan-Southwest and which are tradition-specific.

Key argument: Southwest iconographic programs are not isolated to individual traditions. Kachina cult imagery, warrior symbolism, rain/fertility iconography, and directional symbolism appear across Hohokam, Mogollon, and Ancestral Puebloan contexts, suggesting shared cosmological frameworks that persist across the Classic/post-Classic transition.

## Mimbres and Mogollon Specialists

### J.J. Brody
Art historian and archaeologist. The most prolific writer on Mimbres ceramic imagery.
Primary work: *Mimbres Painted Pottery* (1977, revised 2004, School of American Research Press).

Brody approached Mimbres pottery as an art historical problem — treating the vessels as visual objects with iconographic programs subject to formal and comparative analysis. He documented the full range of figurative and geometric imagery, established the sequence of stylistic development, and argued that the imagery reflects a coherent symbolic system. His framework is the dominant reference point in the field even for scholars who challenge his conclusions.

Key argument: Mimbres figurative imagery is a systematic visual language requiring corpus-level analysis. Individual images must be understood within the full Mimbres corpus before their meaning can be assessed.

### Michelle Hegmon
Archaeologist specializing in social organization and ceramic variability. Applies social-theory frameworks to material culture — ceramic production and distribution as evidence of social organization. Challenges readings that emphasize individual artistic expression. Her framework transfers beyond ceramics to other material categories: any object's formal conventions are social information about the community that produced it.

Key argument: Variability in material culture is not primarily a function of individual choice but of social and organizational factors — production context, distribution networks, community identity.

### Harry Shafer
Production-sequence analysis of Mimbres ceramics. NAN Ranch excavations. Demonstrates that the chaîne opératoire leaves observable evidence at every production stage. His framework transfers to non-ceramic objects: every material has its own production sequence with observable signatures.

Key argument: Artifacts were part of everyday domestic life before becoming mortuary objects. Use wear and production sequence data are primary evidence for function.

### Steven LeBlanc
Demographic and warfare analysis. Classic Mimbres collapse (~1130–1150 CE). Relevant for contextualizing post-Classic material (1150 CE onward) — objects from the post-Classic period exist within a different social landscape than Classic Mimbres material.

### Barbara Moulard
Iconographic narrative sequences in Mimbres figurative imagery. Argues for reading individual images as episodes in broader narratives. Framework applies to any figural program on any medium.

### Darrell Creel
Regional chronology and ceramic typology. Useful for temporal placement of Mimbres-area material across the full sequence.

## Hohokam Specialists

### David Doyel
Regional overview of Hohokam archaeology. The Hohokam tradition (roughly contemporaneous with Mimbres, centered in southern Arizona's river valleys) is distinguished by: platform mounds, ballcourts, extensive irrigation systems, red-on-buff ceramics, and a distinctive shell ornament industry. Doyel's work addresses social organization, regional interaction, and the nature of the Hohokam "regional system."

Key argument: The Hohokam ballcourt network represents a regional integration mechanism — communities participating in shared ritual events across a broad territory. Material culture from ballcourt contexts carries different interpretive weight than domestic assemblages.

### Patricia Crown
Hohokam ceramic production and exchange. Key work: *Ceramics and Ideology: Salado Polychrome Pottery* (1994). Crown demonstrates that the Salado Polychrome horizon (post-Classic, 1275–1450 CE) represents a widely shared ritual/ideological complex transmitted through ceramic conventions, not a population movement. Critical for interpreting post-Classic polychrome ceramics from any Southwest region.

Key argument: Ceramic conventions can carry ideological content that travels faster and farther than populations. Shared material forms indicate shared meanings, not necessarily shared ancestry.

### Emil Haury
Mid-20th century excavator of Snaketown (the type site for Hohokam), Ventana Cave, and other key sites. His reports remain primary references for Hohokam material culture sequences, particularly shell ornament production.

## Ancestral Puebloan / Chaco Specialists

### Stephen Plog
Social complexity and material culture in the Ancestral Puebloan world. Key work: *Ancient Peoples of the American Southwest* (2008). Plog addresses the emergence and collapse of Chaco Canyon as a regional center, and the role of material culture (ceramics, turquoise, great house architecture) in maintaining social hierarchy.

Key argument: Chaco Canyon functioned as a ritual and redistribution center drawing material and labor from a large surrounding region. Objects associated with Chaco contexts — great house deposits, road-adjacent caches — carry different social meaning than domestic assemblages.

### David Wilcox
Southwest regional interaction systems. Addresses how different Southwest traditions interacted, what material evidence marks interaction, and how to distinguish trade goods from locally produced objects in the same style.

## Casas Grandes / Paquimé Specialists

### Paul Minnis and Michael Whalen
Primary excavators and analysts of Paquimé (Casas Grandes), the major late prehistoric center in Chihuahua, Mexico (ca. 1200–1450 CE). Key work: *Casas Grandes and the Ancient Southwest* (2015). Paquimé produced distinctive polychrome ceramics, maintained large-scale macaw and turkey husbandry, and served as a redistribution center for shell and copper objects. Its material culture shows connections to both the U.S. Southwest and Mesoamerica.

Key argument: Paquimé represents a distinct trajectory from U.S. Southwest traditions, not a simple northern extension of Mesoamerican influence. Its material culture requires its own evaluative framework rather than being absorbed into either Southwest or Mesoamerican categories.

## Material Culture and Exchange Specialists

### Kate Spielmann
Craft production, specialization, and exchange in prehistoric Southwest. Addresses who made objects, for whom, under what organizational conditions. Extends Hegmon's social-organization framework to non-ceramic materials and to exchange contexts.

Key argument: Craft production was rarely fully specialized in the prehistoric Southwest. Most objects were produced by part-time specialists or skilled household producers. True full-time specialization is the exception and requires specific evidence (standardization, workshop contexts, large-scale production debris).

### Phil Weigand and Garman Harbottle
Turquoise sourcing in the Southwest. INAA (Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis) and other compositional methods can identify turquoise source mines from the chemical signature of the stone. Without compositional analysis, turquoise source attribution from image analysis alone is not possible — only presence and quality can be assessed.

Key argument: Turquoise distribution in the prehistoric Southwest traces exchange networks precisely. Different source areas (Cerrillos in New Mexico, mines in Nevada, Arizona, Colorado) dominated different periods and regions. The presence of turquoise indicates exchange participation; the specific network requires compositional analysis to identify.

### Kelley Hays-Gillis
Prehistoric Southwest textiles and fiber arts. Documents the full range of weaving, cordage, and basketry traditions across Southwestern cultures. Fiber artifacts survive almost exclusively in dry cave and dry shelter contexts; the absence of fiber from most site assemblages represents a taphonomic bias, not an absence of production.

Key argument: Fiber production was technically sophisticated and socially significant across all Southwest traditions. The rarity of fiber artifacts in the archaeological record is entirely a preservation issue — production was universal, survival is exceptional.

## Methodological Frameworks

### Iconographic analysis (Brody / Schaafsma tradition)
Treats visual imagery as a program to be decoded through formal description, comparative analysis across the corpus, and cross-cultural parallels. Requires describing before interpreting. Contested by processual archaeologists but methodologically indispensable for objects with complex visual programs.

### Technological style / chaîne opératoire
Studies production sequence as social information across all material categories. Every material has a chaîne opératoire — clay selection and forming (ceramics), wood selection and carving (effigies), knapping sequence (lithics), shell processing (ornaments), fiber preparation and construction (textiles). Each sequence encodes learned practice and cultural convention.

### Agency and social identity
Asks what role objects played in constructing social positions — gender, craft specialization, community membership, ritual role. Not unique to ceramics; applies to any category of material culture.

### Exchange and interaction sphere analysis
Studies how objects moved between communities and what that movement means. Key evidence: non-local raw materials (turquoise, shell, obsidian), stylistic conventions shared across distant regions, objects in contexts far from their production areas.

### Cave/cache archaeology
Dry cave environments preserve organic materials absent from open sites. Cache deposits — objects intentionally placed together and left — are analytically distinct from domestic assemblages, midden deposits, or burial assemblages. Caches require their own interpretive framework because their depositional logic is different.

### Compositional analysis (INAA, pXRF, petrography)
Chemical and physical analysis of raw materials to identify source areas. Applies to ceramics (clay and temper sourcing), obsidian (volcanic source identification), turquoise (mine identification), and other materials. Visual analysis alone cannot establish raw material sources.

## Frameworks to Displace

The following evaluative frameworks will attach by default when an LLM analyzes Southwest archaeological material. Each one produces category errors — it measures the work against criteria that are irrelevant or actively wrong for this domain. Name them explicitly in the displacement block so they can be suppressed.

---

### 1. Fine art critical vocabulary

The dominant default. When an LLM sees a painted ceramic vessel, it reaches for the vocabulary of formalist art criticism and museum catalog writing: *tension, ambiguity, productive friction, sustained perceptual reward, compositional innovation, painterly quality, expressive line.* These terms come from Abstract Expressionism, 20th-century formalism, and the critical traditions built around Western fine art.

Applied to Southwest ceramics, this vocabulary misidentifies what is being assessed. When Brody uses "positive-negative ambiguity" for Mimbres geometric painting, he means something specific and technical — the capacity of a design to be read simultaneously as dark-on-light or light-on-dark, a property deliberately built into the system. Fine art "ambiguity" means something different and carries different evaluative weight. The terms look similar; the evaluative logic behind them is not.

**Suppress:** tension, productive friction, sustained perceptual reward, painterly, expressive, and any vocabulary that positions the work as a precursor to or echo of Western fine art movements.

---

### 2. Innovation as a primary criterion

Fine art criticism treats innovation — departure from convention, development of individual style, formal invention — as the highest virtue. An artist who does something new is better than one who does something traditional with excellence.

This is precisely wrong for Southwest archaeological material. Brody is explicit: "conscious striving for radical innovations or highly individualistic personal styles would have been unthinkable" for Mimbres painters. The tradition "permitted innovations only within strictly understood limits." The evaluative achievement is mastery within a constraining system, not escape from it. A Mimbres bowl that perfectly executes a quartered geometric composition with controlled positive-negative ambiguity is a success by the tradition's own standards. Calling it "conservative" or "derivative" imports an evaluative criterion the tradition never held.

**Suppress:** originality, innovation, departure from convention, individual style as positive markers. Replace with: mastery within tradition, line control, adherence to the formal system, and the degree to which the composition achieves the system's specific goals (ambiguity, motion, tension-within-structure in Brody's technical sense).

---

### 3. Individual artistic genius / lone creator frame

Fine art history is organized around individual makers — named artists with identified bodies of work, personal styles, biographical context. The question "who made this?" is central to fine art analysis.

Southwest ceramic production was community-embedded, tradition-bound, and in most cases anonymous. Brody: "the quality of their work was always related to group standards or social ideals." The work of individual potters was recognized by contemporaries through subtle variation within conventional modes — not through departure from them. The production unit was the community and its tradition, not the individual artist.

Applying the lone creator frame produces: attribution speculation, over-reading of individual marks as personal expression, and analysis that focuses on what differentiates a vessel from others rather than on what the vessel achieves within its tradition.

**Suppress:** attribution to individual makers, reading of variation as personal expression, framing of the object as the product of individual vision. Replace with: placement within the tradition, assessment of craft quality relative to community standards, and — where relevant — Brody's three-tier distinction between skilled adult production, child/novice production, and iconographically knowledgeable but technically unskilled production.

---

### 4. The "decoration" frame

When fine art or design criticism encounters painted pattern on a utilitarian object, it categorizes the painting as decoration — surface treatment added to an underlying functional form. This frame positions the iconographic program as secondary and ornamental.

Southwest ceramic painting is not decoration. It is a visual language carrying iconographic content, social meaning, and in the Mimbres case a symbolic program that Brody argues operates at the level of a systematic corpus. Treating it as decoration produces description of surface pattern where analysis of visual meaning is needed.

The practical consequence: a "decoration" frame asks "how does this look?" A visual language frame asks "what does this say, and how well does it say it by the tradition's conventions?"

**Suppress:** decoration, ornamental, surface treatment (as a dismissive category). Replace with: iconographic program, design system, visual language.

---

### 5. Western developmental narrative ("primitive" → "sophisticated")

Western art history is organized around a developmental trajectory — from early/simple/primitive toward later/complex/sophisticated, with the finest Western fine art as the implied endpoint. Applied to indigenous production, this produces: early geometric work read as "primitive," figurative work read as "developed," and the entire tradition read as moving toward something it was not in fact moving toward.

Applied to Mimbres specifically: Style I geometric is not "primitive" relative to Style III figurative. The style sequence reflects an internal logic — technical elaboration of a design system, introduction of new subjects within an existing formal framework — not progress toward a fine art goal. Shafer is explicit that the Mimbres were "not the best ancient potters in the Southwest" technically while simultaneously having "no contemporary rivals" aesthetically. These are independent axes. The developmental narrative collapses them into one scale and gets both wrong.

**Suppress:** primitive, developed, sophisticated (as developmental terms), any framing that positions the tradition as an early stage of something else. Replace with: placement within the tradition's own internal sequence (Style I/II/III for Mimbres), assessment against the tradition's own standards at the relevant period.

---

### 6. Studio craft / contemporary ceramics frame

Contemporary studio pottery criticism values individual voice, deliberate departure from tradition, material experimentation, and the visible mark of individual process. The Shoji Hamada school, the Bernard Leach tradition, and contemporary American studio ceramics all reward these qualities.

Applied to Southwest archaeological ceramics, this frame produces: reading of use-wear as damage (rather than as evidence of domestic function before burial), reading of tradition-conformance as lack of creative ambition, and reading of the formal system's constraints as limitations to be overcome rather than as the medium through which quality is achieved.

**Suppress:** individual voice, studio practice vocabulary, any framing that reads tradition-conformance as a failure of ambition. Replace with: the Mimbres production model (community-embedded, tradition-bound, part-time specialist) and the evaluation of quality within that model.

---

### 7. Ethnographic curiosity frame

A subtler default: treating objects primarily as windows onto cultural "otherness" — interesting because they reveal how different people lived, thought, or believed — rather than as objects with internal aesthetic and functional logic assessable on their own terms.

This frame produces analysis that describes what is culturally distinctive rather than what works or fails by the tradition's own standards. It is also patronizing in a structural way: it positions the objects as data about a culture rather than as things made well or poorly by people who cared about making them well.

**Suppress:** exotic, mysterious, ritual significance (as a catch-all), "windows into" framing. Replace with: the tradition's own evaluative vocabulary, drawn from the corpus. Cultural context is secondary framing, not the primary analytical lens.

## Key Unresolved Debates (Broad Southwest)

1. **Nature of the Chaco system** — Was Chaco Canyon a political center, a ritual center, a redistribution node, or a pilgrimage destination? Evidence supports ritual significance; political hierarchy remains debated.

2. **Hohokam ethnicity and continuity** — Are the historic O'odham (Tohono O'odham, Akimel O'odham) direct descendants of the Hohokam? Archaeological evidence suggests continuity; the relationship is complex and contested.

3. **Casas Grandes and its hinterland** — Was Paquimé a locally emergent center or was it organized by outside influence (Mesoamerican, Chacoan)? Evidence supports local emergence with long-distance connections.

4. **Classic Mimbres collapse** — Warfare/demographic (LeBlanc), environmental, or organizational reorganization? Probably a combination. Post-Classic Mimbres-area material reflects this disruption.

5. **Cave cache interpretation** — Are dry cave deposits ritual caches, storage caches, or abandonment deposits? Context, object type, and assemblage composition all bear on this. The distinction matters for functional interpretation.

6. **Kachina cult origins** — When and where did the Kachina religious complex emerge? Evidence for proto-Kachina imagery appears in rock art and ceramics from ~1275 CE onward across multiple traditions.

7. **Organic material production continuity** — Were wood effigies, basketry, and fiber arts produced throughout the prehistoric period? Almost certainly yes — the absence of pre-cave-context examples is taphonomic, not cultural.
