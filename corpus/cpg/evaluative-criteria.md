# CPG — Evaluative Criteria

Observable criteria for assessing CPG packaging against commercial performance
standards. Organized by criterion type, then by commercial objective routing.

These criteria replace—do not supplement—fine art and design community criteria.

---

## Routing step (required before applying criteria)

Before evaluating, establish:

1. **Commercial objective:** What is this package primarily trying to do?
   - Drive trial (new buyers, new category entrants)
   - Serve loyal buyers (navigation, recognition, reinforcement)
   - Reposition (signal meaningful change while retaining equity)
   - Communicate premium (justify a higher price point)
   - Communicate value/entry (accessibility, category clarity)
   - Launch a new brand (establish assets from zero)

2. **Brand stage:** Is this a legacy brand (has existing distinctive assets to
   protect or evolve) or a new entrant (must establish assets from scratch)?

3. **Category context:** What visual conventions dominate this category?
   Does the package need to conform (for navigation) or disrupt (for attention)?
   Both can be correct—the answer depends on objective and brand stage.

The routing step changes which criteria apply and how they're weighted.

## Criterion 1 — Shelf visibility and category navigation

**The question:** Can a shopper locate this package in its category, at shelf
distance (3–5 feet), within 3 seconds?

Observable indicators:
- Contrast against likely shelf neighbors (lighter or darker, warmer or cooler)
- Size of primary visual element relative to package face
- Category color convention: does the package use, break, or play against it?
- Distinctiveness of shape or structural form at a glance

**Failure conditions:**
- The package reads as any-brand in the category (no distinction from neighbors)
- Category color convention used uncritically, making brand identification dependent
  on reading the wordmark rather than recognizing the visual pattern
- Structural form indistinguishable from competitive set

**Success conditions:**
- Package locatable without relying on reading
- Category membership legible AND brand-specific visual signature present
- In crowded categories: a visual element that creates a "pop" against the set

## Criterion 2 — Brand identification speed

**The question:** Can a shopper who knows this brand identify it within 2 seconds
without reading the wordmark?

This criterion is about what the brand owns beyond its name. A package that requires
reading the name to be identified has weak or absent distinctive assets.

Observable indicators:
- Color that is distinctively owned (not shared with category)
- Shape or form (structural or graphic) that is brand-specific
- Character, symbol, or icon associated with the brand
- Typography style that is recognizable as brand-specific

**Assessment by brand stage:**
- Legacy brand: which existing assets are present, reinforced, or absent?
- New entrant: which visual elements are candidates to become future assets?
  (Are they distinctive enough? Consistently applied? Ownable—not generic premium
  signals that every brand in the category uses?)

**Failure conditions:**
- Brand identification requires reading
- Visual assets are category conventions rather than brand-specific marks
- Redesign has removed or diluted previously established assets (Tropicana model)
- Multiple potential assets present but none dominant enough to own

## Criterion 3 — Communication hierarchy

**The question:** At 3 feet, 2 feet, and in-hand, does the shopper receive
information in the correct sequence—brand first, variant second, benefit third?

Most packaging failures in this criterion are hierarchy problems, not content problems.
The right information is present; it's being read in the wrong order.

Observable hierarchy levels:
- **Level 1 (3 feet):** Brand identity and category placement
- **Level 2 (2 feet):** Variant identification (flavor, size, format)
- **Level 3 (in hand):** Reason to believe, benefit claim, proof

**Failure conditions:**
- Variant name larger or more prominent than brand name (loyalty failure: existing
  buyers can navigate; trial failure: new buyers don't know whose product this is)
- Benefit claim competes with brand ID for primary attention
- Range packaging with variant differentiation so aggressive that brand coherence
  is lost (the package reads as individual products, not a family)
- Hierarchy reversed: benefit first, brand discovered last

**Routing note:** The correct hierarchy varies slightly by objective. A trial-focused
package may appropriately foreground benefit over variant. A loyalty-focused range
may weight variant navigation more heavily. These are exceptions—establish them
explicitly rather than defaulting to them.

## Criterion 4 — Distinctive asset evaluation (Sharp/Romaniuk framework)

**The question:** What does this brand own visually, and does this package
strengthen or weaken that ownership?

Apply the fame-uniqueness matrix to each primary visual element:

| Fame | Uniqueness | Assessment |
|------|-----------|------------|
| High | High | Owned asset—protect, reinforce, never remove without strategic reason |
| High | Low | Shared category asset—using it doesn't differentiate; may be necessary |
| Low | High | Potential future asset—building it deliberately is a choice worth making |
| Low | Low | Generic—low value, consider whether it's earning its place |

**Observable indicators:**
- Color: Is this brand's color distinctively theirs, or used throughout the category?
- Shape: Is the structural form or graphic silhouette recognizable without the name?
- Typography: Is the wordmark or type style recognizably brand-specific?
- Character or symbol: Is there a brand mark beyond the wordmark?
- Structural form: Does the container shape have brand identity independent of graphics?

**Failure conditions (legacy brands):**
- Distinctive asset removed or significantly altered without strategic justification
- Asset replaced with generic category convention (e.g., replacing brand-specific
  color with "premium" off-white used by multiple competitors)
- Range extension that dilutes rather than extends assets

**Failure conditions (new entrants):**
- No distinctive element—everything reads as category convention
- Attempting to own an asset already strongly owned by a competitor
- Inconsistency across variants that prevents asset building

## Criterion 5 — Commercial objective fit

**The question:** Does this package do the right job for its stated objective?

### Trial objective
- Appetite appeal or product visibility (for food/beverage: does it make you
  want to try it?)
- Quality signals appropriate to price point
- Benefit claim clear and credible
- Barrier to trial identified and addressed

### Loyalty objective
- Existing buyers can locate their variant quickly in a range
- Distinctive assets maintained for fast recognition
- No changes that create confusion or "is this still my brand?" doubt

### Repositioning objective
- Retained: the distinctive assets loyal buyers recognize
- Changed: the specific elements that signal the repositioning
- Risk assessment: how much equity is at stake if loyal buyers don't
  recognize the evolved package?

### Premium objective
- Quality signals consistent with price point: materials, finishes, restraint,
  structural form
- Does not rely on fine art aesthetic criteria to communicate premium—relies
  on specific premium category conventions (white space, material quality,
  typography restraint)

### Value/entry objective
- Category membership clear and legible
- Navigation fast and error-free
- Does not attempt to look premium at the expense of category legibility

## What is explicitly NOT a criterion here

These are not failure conditions in CPG packaging evaluation:
- Typographic elegance by design community standards
- Formal innovation or originality
- Aesthetic refinement or sophistication
- Award-worthiness (D&AD, Cannes Lions, Clio, Dieline)
- Designer peer admiration
- Fine art criteria: tension, ambiguity, productive friction, sustained perceptual reward
- Visual complexity as evidence of craft or seriousness

A package can score zero on all of these and perform at the highest commercial level.
Tide's bull's-eye. Morton Salt's umbrella girl. Heinz's keystone bottle.
None of these are design community favorites. All are dominant commercial performers.
