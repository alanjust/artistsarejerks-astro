# CPG — Critics and Frameworks

Three evaluative frameworks exist in CPG packaging. They don't agree.
Understanding all three is necessary—primarily so you know which one to displace.

---

## Framework 1: Brand Strategy / Shopper Performance
*This is the redirect target.*

### Byron Sharp — Ehrenberg-Bass Institute
**Key texts:** *How Brands Grow* (2010), *How Brands Grow Part 2* (2015, with Jenni Romaniuk)
**Institution:** Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, University of South Australia

Core argument: Brands grow by reaching light buyers and non-buyers—the vast
majority of any market—not by deepening loyalty among existing heavy users. Mental
availability (being easy to think of) and physical availability (being easy to find
and buy) are the primary drivers of market share growth.

Key concept — **Distinctive brand assets:** Non-product brand cues (shapes, colors,
characters, logos, slogans) that become reliably associated with the brand through
consistent use over time. Their value is the ability to trigger brand recall without
the brand name being present.

Key concept — **Double jeopardy law:** Brands with lower market share have fewer
buyers who also buy less often. This is a statistical regularity, not a branding
failure. Packaging that helps grow penetration (reach new buyers) matters more than
packaging that rewards loyal buyers.

Evaluative implication: A package succeeds if it reinforces distinctive brand assets,
signals category membership clearly, and enables fast brand identification. Innovation
that erodes distinctive assets is destruction of equity, not design progress.

### Jenni Romaniuk — Ehrenberg-Bass Institute
**Key text:** *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018)

Extends Sharp's framework specifically into visual identity. The core tool is the
**fame-uniqueness matrix:**

- High fame, high uniqueness = owned asset. Protect it.
- High fame, low uniqueness = shared category asset. Using it doesn't help you.
- Low fame, high uniqueness = potential future asset. Worth building.
- Low fame, low uniqueness = weak or generic. Low value.

Evaluative implication: Not all visual elements on a package are equal. Each element
should be assessed for whether it's distinctively owned by the brand vs. shared with
the category. A color that "reads as premium" but is used by all premium brands in
the category is a category cue, not a brand cue.

### Shopper Marketing Research Tradition
**Key institutions:** Path to Purchase Institute, POPAI (Point of Purchase Advertising
International), Nielsen Eye-Tracking Research, Kantar, Ipsos

Key empirical findings:
- Average shelf dwell time in grocery: 3–7 seconds for most categories
- Brand identification must occur within 2 seconds for reliable purchase influence
- Shoppers use peripheral vision and pattern recognition, not close reading
- 70–80% of purchase decisions are made at or near point of purchase (not pre-decided)
- Errors in variant navigation (picking the wrong item) are a common and measurable
  packaging failure—not just inconvenience but a driver of dissatisfaction and returns

## Framework 2: Design Community Critique
*Named here primarily for displacement. This is what to suppress.*

### Key figures
- **Michael Bierut** (Pentagram) — formal excellence, typographic rigor, clarity
- **Paula Scher** (Pentagram) — expressive typography, cultural referencing, scale
- **Abbott Miller** (Pentagram) — information design, editorial sensibility
- **Sagmeister & Walsh** — experiential and emotional design
- **Turner Duckworth** — simplicity, wit, brand distillation

### Award structures (displacement targets)
- D&AD (Design and Art Direction) — UK-based, high prestige in design community
- Clio Awards — advertising and design
- Cannes Lions — Packaging Lions category
- Dieline Awards — packaging-specific, design community judging
- AIGA (American Institute of Graphic Arts) — design community recognition

**Why these are displacement targets:** These award structures reward design
community values—formal elegance, typographic sophistication, conceptual cleverness,
visual innovation. These values may or may not correlate with commercial performance.
The canonical counter-case is Tropicana 2009 (see canonical-works.md): award-caliber
design work that destroyed commercial performance.

---

## Framework 3: Fine Art Critical Vocabulary
*Also a displacement target—the LLM's default.*

Applied to packaging, fine art criteria read virtues as failures:
- A package designed for immediate recognition reads as "lacking subtlety"
- Clear category signaling reads as "generic" or "unambitious"
- Distinctive color blocking reads as "unsophisticated"
- Legibility-first typography reads as "artistically uninventive"

This framework enters through the LLM's training data. Suppress it explicitly.

---

## The Three-Framework Tension

A package can win on Framework 2 (design community) while failing on Framework 1
(commercial performance). The Tropicana redesign did exactly this. A package can
score poorly on Framework 2 while dominating on Framework 1—Tide's bull's-eye
is not a design community favorite; it is one of the most recognizable packages
in American retail and has been for decades.

Framework 3 (fine art) is almost entirely irrelevant to commercial packaging
evaluation and should be suppressed before either of the other two frameworks
is applied.
