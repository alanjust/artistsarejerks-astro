## Byron Sharp — *How Brands Grow* (2010)

**On mental availability:**
"Mental availability refers to the probability that a buyer will notice, recognise
and/or think of a brand in buying situations. It depends on the quantity and quality
of memory structures related to the brand."

**On distinctive assets:**
"Distinctive assets are brand elements—other than the brand name—that through
consistent use over time have become strongly linked to the brand in memory. They
can be colors, logos, taglines, shapes, characters, and so on."

**On what drives growth:**
"It's penetration, not loyalty, that matters most to a brand's success. Growing a
brand means reaching buyers who currently don't buy the brand, or who buy it only
rarely. This is the law of double jeopardy."

**On consistency:**
"Brands that consistently use the same set of distinctive assets build stronger
mental structures than those that refresh, reinvent, or update their visual identity
regularly. Change has a cost. That cost is the erosion of mental availability."

**On the function of packaging:**
"The pack's job is primarily to get noticed (be visible on shelf), be identified
(link to the brand quickly), and not confuse (variant navigation). Secondary to
this is any communicative work the pack might do about product benefits."

## Jenni Romaniuk — *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018)

**On the fame-uniqueness matrix:**
"An asset's value comes from two qualities: how well-known it is among buyers in
the category, and how uniquely it is associated with one brand rather than the
category as a whole. Fame without uniqueness is a category cue. Uniqueness without
fame is an undiscovered opportunity."

**On color:**
"Color is the most powerful distinctive asset available to a brand because it
operates in peripheral vision and at speed. But most brands don't own a color—
they share one with the category. The question is not 'does this color look good'
but 'does this color belong to us.'"

**On the cost of change:**
"Every time a brand changes or removes a distinctive asset, it destroys some of
the mental availability that asset represented. If the asset has high fame and
high uniqueness, the cost of removing it is very high. Brands often underestimate
this cost because the mental availability built by an asset is invisible until
it's gone."

**On what makes an element a candidate asset:**
"Consistency, distinction from competitors, and memorability. An element that
is used consistently, doesn't appear on competitors' packs, and is easy to
encode and retrieve in memory is a candidate distinctive asset. An element
that is beautiful, sophisticated, or award-winning is not necessarily any of
these things."

## Shopper marketing findings (compiled from industry research)

**On dwell time:**
"Average shopper dwell time at shelf in grocery is 3–7 seconds across most
categories. In high-purchase-frequency categories (dairy, beverages, snacks),
it is often less. Package assessment happens in a fraction of the time a designer
spends evaluating a comp." — Path to Purchase Institute, 2019

**On the 2-second rule:**
"For established brands, brand identification should occur within 2 seconds of
a shopper reaching the category. If it takes longer, the package is doing extra
work that may not get done." — Nielsen Eye-Tracking Research

**On variant navigation errors:**
"In complex ranges, variant navigation errors—picking the wrong size, flavor, or
format—are a measurable and significant packaging failure. These errors drive
complaints, returns, and brand dissatisfaction. A package that is individually
beautiful but creates errors within a range has failed at a basic function."
— Ipsos Shopper Research

**On peripheral vision:**
"Shoppers do not read packages. They recognize patterns. A package designed for
reading is designed for a shopper who doesn't exist in the shopping context."
— POPAI Research

## The Tropicana case — documented record

**Timeline (from public reporting, 2009):**
- January 2009: New Tropicana Pure Premium package launched, designed by Arnell Group
- Design changes: Straw-in-orange image removed; replaced with glass of juice.
  Cap changed from distinctive orange "fruit" cap to plain round cap in juice color.
  Wordmark reoriented vertically.
- February 2009: Sales reported down approximately 20% versus prior year period.
  Consumer complaints described as "unprecedented" in volume.
- February 23, 2009: PepsiCo announces revert to original packaging.
- March 2009: Original packaging back on shelf.

**Estimated loss:** Approximately $30 million in sales over 6–8 weeks.

**Why it failed (Sharp's post-hoc analysis):**
The straw-in-orange image was a distinctive brand asset with high fame and high
uniqueness. It identified Tropicana at distance and at speed without requiring
the shopper to read the wordmark. Removing it required shoppers to read—in a
context where shoppers don't read. The new glass-of-juice image was an accurate
product depiction but shared with multiple competitors. It provided no brand
identification advantage.

**Design community response at launch:** Generally positive. The new design was
described as "cleaner," "more modern," and "more premium." These are design
community evaluative criteria. None of them predicted commercial performance.

## Key vocabulary from this tradition

Terms to use in CPG evaluation (use these, not fine art vocabulary):

- **Mental availability** — the probability a buyer will think of this brand
- **Physical availability** — how easy the brand is to find and buy
- **Distinctive brand asset** — a non-name cue reliably associated with the brand
- **Fame** — how widely known an asset is among category buyers
- **Uniqueness** — how distinctively the asset is associated with one brand vs. category
- **Asset strength** — the combined measure of fame and uniqueness
- **Penetration** — the number of buyers (primary growth lever)
- **Double jeopardy** — the empirical law: smaller brands have fewer, lighter buyers
- **Mental structure** — the memory associations that make a brand easy to think of
- **Category entry point** — the buying situation or need that triggers brand consideration
- **Shelf navigation** — how quickly a shopper can locate their intended purchase
- **Variant navigation** — how quickly a shopper can identify the correct SKU within a range
- **Dwell time** — the seconds a shopper spends in front of a category
- **Salience** — standing out in the buying context

Terms to avoid (these belong to other frameworks):
- Tension, ambiguity, friction (fine art)
- Elegance, sophistication, refinement (design community)
- Restraint, wit, concept (design community)
- Timeless, classic, enduring (these mean different things in each framework—
  in Sharp's terms, "timeless" means asset consistency, not aesthetic quality)
