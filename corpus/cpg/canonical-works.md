# CPG — Canonical Works

The reference class for CPG packaging evaluation. Organized by what each
case demonstrates—not by aesthetic quality, but by what it proves about
commercial performance and distinctive asset management.

---

## High-performing packages — market performance reference class

### Heinz Ketchup
**What it proves:** Long-term distinctive asset consistency across format changes.
The keystone bottle shape, the red, the Heinz script wordmark, the "57" mark—all
maintained across decades of redesign and format extension (glass → plastic →
squeeze → sachet). Each individual element is ownable and recognizable without
the others. Benchmark for asset discipline.

### Tide (Procter & Gamble)
**What it proves:** Distinctive asset protection under design pressure. The
orange/yellow bull's-eye is not a design community favorite. It is one of the
most recognizable packages in American retail. P&G has resisted pressure to
modernize it into something more design-forward for decades. The bull's-eye
identifies at distance, at speed, in peripheral vision. Benchmark for resisting
design community pressure when commercial performance is strong.

### Coca-Cola
**What it proves:** The contour bottle as structural distinctive asset; red as
owned category color; script wordmark at scale. Coca-Cola's asset stack is
the most studied in marketing. The contour bottle is recognizable as a silhouette,
in the dark, broken in half. This is the upper bound of distinctive asset strength.

### Heineken
**What it proves:** Green + red star + specific bottle shape as a cross-market
distinctive asset. Operates across significant cultural variation without
local adaptation undermining the asset stack. Benchmark for global asset
coherence.

### Kind Bar
**What it proves:** Structural solution to a communication problem. The
transparency window makes the product itself the hero—the ingredient story
is not told in copy but shown through the package structure. The design
solves a credibility problem (natural ingredients) by making it unambiguously
visible. Benchmark for when structural innovation serves a specific communication
objective rather than aesthetic ambition.

### Method Soap
**What it proves:** The counter-case where design community values and commercial
performance aligned. Method's distinctive bottle forms earned design community
admiration AND drove trial in a category (household cleaning) where packaging
had been entirely functional. Worth studying as the exception—what made it work
commercially was that the design served a specific positioning (premium/natural
in a commodity category) and the distinctive form created category disruption
at the right moment. Not a template—a case study in alignment.

### Innocent Drinks (UK)
**What it proves:** Personality-driven packaging as a brand-building tool in a
commodity category. Hand-drawn type, conversational copy, approachable visual
warmth—these built distinctive brand equity in UK grocery where smoothies were
otherwise undifferentiated. The design matched the brand voice precisely.
Benchmark for personality-as-asset.

### Morton Salt
**What it proves:** Simplicity and consistency over time. The girl with the
umbrella, the blue, "When it rains it pours"—maintained since 1914. Not a
sophisticated design. An enormously powerful distinctive asset built through
consistency alone. Benchmark for the compounding value of not changing things
that work.

## Instructive failures — what went wrong and why

### Tropicana (2009) — The canonical cautionary case
**What happened:** Arnell Group (commissioned by PepsiCo) redesigned the
Tropicana Pure Premium package. The redesign removed the iconic straw-in-orange
image and replaced it with a generic glass of juice. The distinctive orange cap
was replaced with a round cap in juice-color variants. The wordmark was
reoriented vertically.

**Design community response:** Generally positive. Cleaner, more modern,
more premium-feeling by conventional design standards.

**Market response:** Sales dropped approximately 20% in six weeks—roughly
$30 million in lost revenue. Consumer complaints were immediate and widespread.
PepsiCo reverted the packaging within two months.

**What it demonstrates:** The straw-in-orange image was a distinctive brand asset
with more recognition power than anyone at PepsiCo had measured before destroying
it. The redesign optimized for design community criteria while eliminating the
primary asset that drove brand identification at shelf. This is the case Byron
Sharp cites most frequently. Use it as the reference case for the cost of
distinctive asset erosion.

### Gap Logo (2010)
**What happened:** Gap replaced its 20-year-old blue square/Spencerian script
logo with a new design (Helvetica wordmark with a small blue gradient square
in the corner). The redesign lasted 7 days before being reverted following
consumer outcry.

**What it demonstrates:** Consumer attachment to established distinctive assets
is often stronger—and more commercially significant—than brand teams anticipate.
The old logo was not a great design by contemporary standards. It was an
enormously powerful distinctive asset. The new logo was competently designed.
It owned nothing.

### Tropicana and Gap together
These two cases, both 2009–2010, are the clearest paired demonstration that
design community evaluation criteria and commercial performance criteria can
point in opposite directions. Both redesigns would have improved under design
community assessment. Both failed commercially. Both were reverted.

## Cases requiring nuance

### Aesop
Design community admiration is high. Commercial performance is real but niche.
The question for this case is whether the design drives performance or whether
the performance is driven by positioning, distribution, and price point—and
the design is downstream of those decisions rather than driving them.
Do not use as a general benchmark. Use as a premium niche case study only.

### Apple Packaging
Often cited as premium packaging design done right. Caveat: Apple's packaging
works because it is integrated with an entire retail experience and an extremely
high-equity brand. The packaging is not doing the work alone. Not a transferable
template for brands without equivalent retail control and brand equity.
