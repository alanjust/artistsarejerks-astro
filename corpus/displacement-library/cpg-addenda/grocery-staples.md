## Category context — Grocery staples

### What this category is

Grocery staples covers the high-frequency, habit-driven purchases that
make up the backbone of a grocery basket: condiments (Heinz, French's,
Hellmann's), canned goods (Campbell's, Del Monte, Goya), baking (Gold
Medal, King Arthur, C&H), dairy (Tillamook, Land O'Lakes, Kraft),
household cleaning (Tide, Dawn, Lysol), paper goods (Bounty, Charmin,
Quilted Northern), and pantry essentials (Morton Salt, Arm & Hammer,
McCormick spices).

These are categories where brand switching is low, purchase frequency is
high, and the primary packaging job is fast, reliable recognition for a
shopper who has bought this product dozens or hundreds of times.

### Dominant visual conventions

**Color:** Color blocking is the dominant visual language. Bold, high-contrast
color fields — often a single dominant color associated with the brand —
make these packages identifiable in peripheral vision. Heinz red. Tide orange.
Dawn blue. Morton blue. These are not accidental color choices; they are
the result of decades of conditioning.

**Typography:** Large, bold, high-contrast wordmarks. Legibility at distance
and at speed is the primary typographic job. Typographic elegance is
secondary to recognition. The wordmark often IS the distinctive asset.

**Structure:** These packages tend toward convention rather than disruption.
Shoppers navigate by pattern recognition; an unusual structure creates
friction rather than distinction in a repeat-purchase category.

**Claims:** Benefit claims are present but secondary. In grocery staples,
the brand IS the benefit claim for loyal buyers. "New and improved" and
variant callouts matter for communication to existing buyers; trial-driving
claims are less important because the category relies on habit and conversion
from within the brand's buyer base.

**Imagery:** Product photography appears in food categories (appetite appeal)
but is often smaller and less dominant than in premium food. The brand mark
and product name often dominate the primary face.

### The stability imperative in this category

This is the category where distinctive asset protection matters most.
Grocery staple buyers navigate by habit. They're not comparing products at
shelf — they're executing a routine. A significant visual change disrupts
that routine and forces a re-evaluation they may resolve by switching brands.

The Tropicana 2009 case (referenced in the base block) is the canonical
grocery staple packaging failure. The relevant lesson: in this category,
the threshold for visual change is very high. Evaluate any redesign
against the question: "Will existing buyers still find this automatically,
without pausing?"

### Where category conventions matter less

Premium grocery (organic, specialty, artisan) operates by partially different
conventions. A premium olive oil or specialty pasta brand may use more
restrained, editorial visual language — borrowed from the premium/DTC world —
because its buyer is spending more time per purchase and self-selects into
premium cues. Note when you are evaluating a premium grocery product and
adjust accordingly.

## Category reference class — Grocery staples

These replace the generic CPG reference class from the base block for this
category.

**Benchmark performers**

**Heinz Ketchup** — the primary benchmark for this entire category. The
keystone bottle, the red, the Heinz script, the "57" mark — maintained
across format changes (glass to plastic to squeeze to single-serve) for
over 150 years. Shoppers identify Heinz before they read the word "ketchup."
Benchmark for distinctive asset discipline across time and format.

**Tide (P&G)** — orange/yellow bull's-eye. Not a design community favorite.
One of the most recognizable packages in American retail. Has withstood
significant pressure to "modernize" because P&G understands the bull's-eye
is worth more than any aesthetic improvement. Benchmark for resisting
design community pressure when commercial performance is strong.

**Morton Salt** — the umbrella girl and blue oval, unchanged in essential
form since 1914. "When it rains it pours." A distinctive asset built
entirely through repetition rather than design innovation. Benchmark for
the compounding value of consistency.

**Campbell's Soup** — red and white, the Spencerian script, the medal.
Andy Warhol's appropriation didn't hurt the brand; it confirmed the brand's
cultural embeddedness. Multiple line extensions managed without losing the
core asset stack. Benchmark for range extension that maintains brand coherence.

**Arm & Hammer** — the baking soda arm-and-hammer logo maintained across
category expansion (toothpaste, detergent, deodorant, cat litter). The
same distinctive asset serves across wildly different product categories.
Benchmark for asset portability across category expansion.

**Dawn Dish Soap** — blue ownership in dish soap. Grease-cutting credential
communicated through consistent color and "tough on grease, gentle on hands"
narrative. Benchmark for single-color dominance in a category.

**Counter-case**

**Tropicana (2009)** — see base block. The canonical grocery staples failure.
Critical to understand this case before evaluating any legacy grocery staple
brand facing redesign.

**Generic/private label** — useful as a reference for what category-convention
conformity looks like at its least differentiated. When a national brand's
packaging reads like private label, it has lost its distinctive assets.

## Category routing notes

**Commercial objective:** Grocery staples are almost always evaluated for:
- Serve loyal buyers (dominant — navigation, recognition, reinforcement)
- Reposition (when a brand is modernizing or expanding)

Trial-driving objectives are less common in established grocery staple brands.
If the brief is trial-focused, note it explicitly — it's the exception.

**Brand stage:** Assume legacy brand unless specifically briefed otherwise.
Most grocery staple brands have 20–100+ years of packaging equity. Treat
any grocery staple brand with significant national distribution as a legacy
brand.

**Variant navigation complexity:** Many grocery staple brands manage large
ranges (multiple flavors, sizes, formats). Variant navigation — can the
shopper find the right SKU without confusion — is an active evaluation
criterion in this category. Note the range size when known.

**Retailer context matters:** A package evaluated for Walmart (high volume,
fast navigation, value signals) reads differently than the same product
in Whole Foods (slower browse, premium signals expected). Note the primary
retailer context if known.
