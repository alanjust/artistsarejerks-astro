## Category context — Premium spirits

### Dominant visual conventions

The premium spirits shelf is one of the most visually dense and
convention-driven in retail. Key conventions to know:

**Color:** Dark backgrounds dominate — black, deep navy, forest green, rich
burgundy. White and off-white appear in ultra-premium and Japanese whisky
categories. Metallics (gold, copper, silver foil) are near-universal for
premium tier; their absence is often a deliberate signal of restraint and
confidence.

**Typography:** Serif typefaces dominate, particularly traditional forms
(transitional serifs, slab serifs, engraver-style lettering). Script and
hand-lettered elements are common for heritage brands. Sans-serif is used
for contemporary or craft positioning, but rarely as the primary wordmark
in premium tier.

**Structural conventions:** Tall, narrow bottles with substantial weight
are category convention. Embossing, debossing, and tactile label stock are
expected at premium price points. Wax seals, cork closures, and capsule
details are active premium signals. Low-fill bottles (headspace as signal
of small-batch craft) appear in craft/artisan positioning.

**Label conventions:** Multi-element labels are common — primary label,
back label, neck label, and often foil or embossed elements layered on top.
Heritage credentials (founding date, distillery location, production method)
are typically present. Handwritten batch/bottle numbers appear in limited-
edition and single-barrel expressions.

**Imagery:** Crest and heraldic elements common in Scotch whisky tradition.
Landscape and origin imagery (agave fields, highland lochs, copper stills)
used for authenticity signaling. Minimal or abstract imagery used for
ultra-premium and contemporary positioning.

### Where category conventions break down

Craft and artisan spirits increasingly disrupt category conventions as a
positioning strategy:
- Bright colors on light backgrounds (against dark premium convention)
- Illustration-forward labels (against heritage typography convention)
- Unconventional bottle forms (against tall-narrow convention)

When evaluating a disruptor, assess whether the disruption is legible as
intentional positioning or reads as category ignorance. The former can
succeed; the latter typically fails.

## Category reference class — Premium spirits

These replace the generic CPG reference class from the base block for this
category. Use these as the comparison set.

**Benchmark performers — established distinctive asset owners**

**The Macallan** — amber/gold color ownership in single malt Scotch;
distinctive estate imagery; consistent multi-label structure across decades.
Benchmark for heritage asset consistency in premium spirits.

**Johnnie Walker (Black Label and above)** — the striding man icon and
diagonal label are two of the most powerful distinctive assets in spirits.
The diagonal label was originally functional (bottle identification when
cases were stacked); it became a brand asset through consistent use.
Benchmark for structural-element-as-asset.

**Hendrick's Gin** — dark apothecary bottle form, Victorian illustration
style, cucumber-and-rose brand narrative expressed visually. Succeeded by
disrupting the clear-bottle gin category convention. Benchmark for category
disruption that is legible as intentional positioning.

**Clase Azul Tequila** — hand-painted ceramic decanter as bottle. The
packaging IS the product at a price point where the bottle is kept after
the tequila is gone. Benchmark for structural premium where the container
is a significant portion of the value proposition.

**Hibiki Japanese Whisky (Suntory)** — 24-facet bottle form representing
the 24 seasons of the Japanese calendar; blended whisky category in Japan.
The structural form carries the brand story. Benchmark for structural
distinctive asset with cultural specificity.

**Buffalo Trace** — restrained, classic label design in a category where
labels often overclaim. The restraint IS the premium signal. Benchmark for
confidence through understatement.

**Counter-case**

**Fireball** — successful at its price point and objective, but not a
reference for premium positioning. Aggressive color, character-driven
label, broad retail placement. Do not use as premium spirits benchmark
regardless of its commercial success.

## Category routing notes

**Commercial objective:** In premium spirits, the dominant objectives are:
- Communicate premium (most common) — justify price point through visual quality signals
- Reposition (common for legacy brands modernizing without losing equity)
- Launch new brand (common in craft/artisan tier — must establish assets from zero)
- Drive trial is less common in premium tier; sampling programs do this work,
  not packaging

**Brand stage:** Premium spirits skew legacy. Many brands are 10–100+ years
old with significant distinctive asset equity. Treat any brand over 15 years
old as a legacy brand unless the brief specifically indicates otherwise.

**Price tier matters:** Evaluate against the appropriate tier reference class.
A $25 bottle is not competing against The Macallan. Establish the retail
price tier in the routing step if known.
